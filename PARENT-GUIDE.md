# A Safeguarding Guide for Parents and Guardians

This is a short, plain-language guide for parents, guardians, and anyone who cares for a child. It summarizes what the research in this repository supports, in the form you need at home. The full evidence, with citations, is in [`research/03-grooming.md`](research/03-grooming.md), [`research/05-statistics.md`](research/05-statistics.md), and [`research/06-prevention.md`](research/06-prevention.md).

Two things to know before you read on:

- **Nothing here identifies an offender in advance.** There is no reliable profile, appearance, body type, income level, or family form that predicts who will abuse a child.
- **Responsibility rests with the adult who offends and with the institutions that give that adult access.** It does not rest with the child, and it does not rest with you for failing to spot a "type."

What you can influence is **access, supervision, and how safe your child feels telling you things**. That is where this guide spends its effort.

---

## The single most useful fact

Most child sexual abuse is committed by someone the child or family knows and trusts. CDC estimates about 90%. Grooming is built to look like good character: the offender is often the most helpful, patient, and generous adult in the child's life. That is the method, not an accident.

The practical consequence is that safety comes from how you arrange access and supervision, not from how confident you feel about someone's personality.

---

## 1. Map who has access to your child

"Access" means any situation where an adult is alone with your child, or has a private channel to them. Access is not a personality trait, so it can be listed and reviewed.

The main routes to check:

- **Home and family:** relatives, family friends, lodgers, partners, and older siblings' friends.
- **Care:** babysitters, nannies, au pairs, and after-school care.
- **Activities:** coaches, tutors, instructors, youth leaders, and clergy.
- **Transport:** who drives your child, and whether anyone else is in the car.
- **Overnight stays:** camps, sleepovers, trips, and tournaments.
- **Health and therapy:** appointments where you are not present.
- **Digital:** anyone who can message your child privately, on any platform.

Questions worth asking, of yourself and of any organization your child attends:

1. Who is alone with my child, and when?
2. Is a second adult present, or is the space openly visible?
3. Who handles toileting, changing, bathing, discipline, and medical care?
4. Who can message my child privately, and on which platform?
5. What are the rules about gifts, money, and special one-child privileges?
6. Who is the safeguarding lead, and what happens when I raise a concern?

What this looks like in practice:

- Keep a short list of adults with one-to-one access, and review it when activities change.
- Drop in unannounced sometimes, at anything your child attends.
- Ask about the safeguarding policy before you enroll, not after.
- Prefer settings with open visibility and a two-adult rule.
- Treat "we do not have a policy" as information worth weighing, not automatically as proof of danger.
- Notice who resists ordinary questions, or who wants to be the only gateway to your child's social life.

**A note on family structure.** Single-parent households, stepfamilies, and families under financial stress appear more often in some datasets because of supervision and access, not because of character. Support is the useful response, not suspicion.

**A note on the viral claim.** The idea that offenders target children whose fathers weigh under 200 pounds has no empirical support. It is traced and refuted in [`research/04-father-protection-theory.md`](research/04-father-protection-theory.md). Body size is not a safeguarding variable.

## 2. Grooming warning signs

Grooming is a pattern, not a moment. These signs are probabilistic: none proves abuse, and none should be treated as a diagnosis. They are reasons to observe, ask, and consult, not to accuse.

**In an adult's behavior toward a child:**

- Unusual interest in a particular child, their body, development, or sexual knowledge.
- Repeatedly arranging one-to-one time, especially overnight or out of sight.
- Gifts, money, or privileges without a clear reason, or gifts paired with requests for secrecy.
- Boundary violations: tickling, wrestling, lap-sitting, massages, sharing a bed, changing in front of the child.
- Heavy private contact by phone, messaging, or games, especially late at night.
- Discouraging the child from friends or family, or building a "you and me against them" frame.
- Treating the child as a peer or romantic partner, or calling the child "mature for their age."
- Defensiveness, anger, or legal threats in response to ordinary safeguarding questions.
- Refusing boundary policies, or seeking settings with weak oversight.

**In a child's behavior:**

- Sudden withdrawal, regression, anxiety, aggression, or new fear of a place or person.
- Sleep problems, nightmares, or distress around a specific adult or activity.
- Sexual knowledge, language, or behavior out of step with the child's age.
- Unexplained gifts, money, or devices.
- Secrecy about online contacts, or distress after using a device.
- Self-harm, substance use, or talk of suicide.
- Physical signs such as pain, bleeding, or bruising in genital or anal areas need medical assessment, not a conclusion drawn at home.

**In an online context:**

- An older "friend" the child is secretive about.
- Requests for images, video calls, or meetings.
- Pressure to move to a private or disappearing-message app.
- Gifts, game currency, or money from an online contact.
- Threats, blackmail, or panic after a notification.

One caution matters more than the rest: the relationship often looks warm and generous. "Good with kids" is not a safety signal. Audit behavior and access, not reputation.

## 3. Digital safety

- **Know the platforms.** Games, chat apps, and social media all include private messaging. Ask which ones your child uses and how messaging works on each.
- **Set privacy settings together.** Defaults are not safe defaults, and doing it together teaches the settings.
- **Agree on device rules.** Charging outside bedrooms, no devices in bathrooms, and a family agreement about what gets shared and with whom.
- **Talk about images before they are sent.** A sent image cannot be recalled. No adult or older peer needs a picture of a child's body.
- **Make telling you the safe option.** The most protective factor is a child who will bring you a scary or embarrassing online interaction without fearing punishment.
- **Do not take the device away because of a disclosure.** A child who loses their phone for telling will not tell again. Respond to the offender, not the child.

**If a child is being blackmailed with an image (sextortion):**

1. **Stop contact.** Do not pay, because payment usually increases demands.
2. **Preserve evidence** before blocking: screenshots of messages, usernames, profile links, and payment requests.
3. **Report** to the platform, to NCMEC's CyberTipline at report.cybertip.org, and to local police.
4. **Get support the same day.** Sextortion is associated with severe shame and with suicide risk, particularly among teenage boys.
5. **Do not delete the account** until evidence is preserved. Deactivation may be reasonable after reporting.

The child here is the victim of a crime, not a rule-breaker who needs a lecture about screen time.

## 4. If a child tells you

Most children delay disclosure or never disclose. A U.S. national sample found that 66.3% of episodes among 10 to 17 year olds were not reported to a parent or any adult, and police reports occurred in only 19.1% of cases. A U.K. review found that over 70% of victims do not tell within five years and about 1 in 5 never tell. A child who tells you years later, or in fragments, or while minimizing what happened, is behaving normally.

What to do:

1. **Stay calm.** Your reaction becomes the child's memory of telling.
2. **Believe and thank them.** "Thank you for telling me. I believe you. This is not your fault."
3. **Do not interrogate.** Ask only what you need to keep them safe, because detailed questioning can compromise a forensic interview later.
4. **Do not promise secrecy.** Say that you will tell someone who can help keep them safe, and tell them who that is.
5. **Make them safe now, and report.** Contact child protection services or police, or a hotline if you are unsure where to start.
6. **Get support** for the child and for yourself.

What to avoid:

- Showing shock, anger, or disgust at the child.
- Asking why they did not tell you sooner.
- Making them repeat the account to several relatives.
- Confronting the person with the child present.
- Posting about it on social media, even in a private group.

## 5. If you are worried about an adult

- Write down what you observed in behavioral terms, with dates.
- Do not investigate or confront the person alone.
- Report to the organization's safeguarding lead and, where the law requires it, to child protection services or police.
- Do not warn the person or give them a chance to "explain" before a report is made.
- A report is a request for an investigation, not an accusation. Good-faith reports are protected in many jurisdictions.

---

## 6. What does not help

- **"Stranger danger" as the main message.** Most abuse is by known persons, and stranger-focused warnings can make a child less likely to disclose abuse by a trusted adult.
- **Judging a father by body weight.** No evidence supports it, and it offers false reassurance.
- **Relying on a background check as a guarantee.** Checks catch some prior convictions. They do not predict a first offense.
- **Gut feeling alone.** Intuition is shaped by reputation and bias. Use it to prompt a question, not to close one.
- **Taking away a device after a disclosure.** This punishes the disclosure.
- **Teaching a child that prevention is their job.** Children are taught obedience to adults and are often emotionally bound to the offender. Prevention is an adult responsibility.

## Where to get help

In the United States:

- **Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline:** 1-800-422-4453
- **NCMEC:** 1-800-THE-LOST (1-800-843-5678)
- **RAINN:** 800-656-4673
- **Emergency:** 911

Outside the United States, contact your local child protection service or police. If you are a survivor, you are not required to read any of this, and support is available.
