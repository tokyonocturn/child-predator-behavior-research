# Child Predator Behavior Research

An evidence-based, public research repository on how and why adults sexually abuse children, and what actually protects children.

**Purpose.** This project synthesizes peer-reviewed research, meta-analyses, and government data to explain victim selection, offender psychology, grooming, prevalence, and prevention. It is written for parents, guardians, educators, coaches, clinicians, policymakers, journalists, and students. It is also written to correct a specific, widespread piece of misinformation: the claim that predators select children based on their father's body weight.

**What this is not.** This is not a profile, a risk-assessment instrument, a screening checklist for individuals, or an operational guide. It contains no instructions for evading detection or manipulating children. Descriptions of offender behavior are included only at the level of abstraction needed for recognition and prevention.

**If you are a parent or guardian, start here:** [`PARENT-GUIDE.md`](PARENT-GUIDE.md) is a short, plain-language safeguarding guide covering access mapping, grooming warning signs, digital safety, and how to respond to a disclosure.

## Bottom line up front

1. **Most child sexual abuse is committed by someone the child or family knows.** CDC estimates about 90% of child sexual abuse is perpetrated by someone known and trusted by the child or family. "Stranger danger" addresses a minority of cases.
2. **There is no single predator profile.** Offenders differ in motivation, age preference, relationship to the child, and method. Pedophilia (a sexual interest in prepubescent children) is diagnosed in 27% to 70% of people who have offended against a child, depending on sample and assessment method, so a substantial share are not preferentially attracted to children, and most people with pedophilia never come to official attention. Risk comes from behavior and opportunity, not from appearance or demographic category.
3. **Grooming is a process of access, trust, testing, isolation, and secrecy, not a single act.** It targets the child *and* the adults around the child. The same behaviors that make a person trusted can be the grooming behaviors.
4. **The "father weighs under 200 pounds" claim is unsupported.** No peer-reviewed study measures paternal body weight as a selection criterion. The claim appears to be a viral distortion of anecdotal statements by investigators and one convicted offender, whose actual point was about perceived guardianship and availability, not physical size. See [`research/04-father-protection-theory.md`](research/04-father-protection-theory.md).
5. **Prevention works best when it changes environments, not just children's knowledge.** School-based programs reliably improve knowledge and self-protection skills; evidence for lasting behavioral change is weaker. Situational prevention (reducing isolation, controlling access, enforcing boundaries, and creating capable guardians) has the strongest practical rationale for organizations and families.
6. **Disclosure is the exception, not the norm.** Most victims delay disclosure for years or never disclose. A child who does not report is behaving normally, not lying or complicit. See [`research/05-statistics.md`](research/05-statistics.md).

## How evidence is graded in this repository

Every substantive claim is tagged or discussed using one of four levels. The full rubric is in [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md).

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Established** | Replicated across independent samples, supported by systematic reviews or meta-analyses, or consistent national surveillance data |
| **Emerging** | Supported by a small number of studies, one dataset, qualitative research, or a new measurement approach; promising but not settled |
| **Disputed** | Credible studies conflict, measures are contested, or the finding depends heavily on definitions and method |
| **Unsupported** | No credible empirical support, or contradicted by the available evidence. Includes myths and viral claims |

Correlation is described as correlation. Where a factor is a marker rather than a cause, the text says so. Where research cannot distinguish competing explanations, the text says that too.

## A note on language and stigma

- **"Pedophile" is not a synonym for "child molester."** Pedophilia is a clinical pattern of sexual interest; child sexual abuse is a behavior. Conflating them produces bad policy and bad conversations. This repository uses "person who sexually abused a child" or "offender" for behavior, and "pedophilia" only for the clinical construct.
- **Risk factors are not blame.** A child's family structure, disability, or prior victimization may be statistically associated with elevated risk. That does not make the child or the family responsible for an adult's choice to offend. Responsibility always rests with the offender.
- **Demographic stereotypes are not evidence.** Offenders are not concentrated in a single race, class, religion, sexual orientation, or family form. Where base rates differ, they track opportunity, reporting, and structural factors, not group character.
- **Victimization is not destiny.** Most survivors do not go on to offend. Claims that "abused children become abusers" are both empirically overstated and harmful.

## Using this repository responsibly

- Do not use this material to profile or accuse a specific person. There is no reliable checklist that identifies an offender in advance.
- Do not use it to justify restricting a child's contact with a parent, relative, or community on the basis of body size, income, or family structure.
- If you are concerned about a specific child, contact a qualified professional or the appropriate authority. In the United States: **Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline, 1-800-422-4453**; **NCMEC, 1-800-THE-LOST (1-800-843-5678)**; **RAINN, 800-656-4673**. Emergency: **911**.
- If you are a survivor, you are not required to read this. Materials about abuse can be activating. Support is available at the numbers above.

## Scope and limitations

This review prioritizes English-language, peer-reviewed research and official data from the CDC, FBI, DOJ/BJS, NCMEC, WHO, and national prevalence studies, with additional Australian and UK sources where they provide the best available estimates. It is not a systematic review with a registered protocol. It does not adjudicate individual cases, provide clinical assessment, or offer legal advice. Prevalence estimates vary substantially by definition, sampling, and disclosure method; the repository reports ranges and flags heterogeneity rather than presenting a single "true" number.

Full limitations, including publication bias, underreporting, cross-sectional designs, and the difficulty of generalizing from convicted samples, are in [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md).
