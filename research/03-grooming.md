# Grooming: Manipulation, Trust, Secrecy, and Warning Signs

**Evidence grade summary:** Grooming as a process preceding and maintaining abuse is **Established**. The five-stage model is **Established as a descriptive framework**, not a fixed sequence. Specific tactic frequencies from survivor and offender samples are **Emerging**. The effectiveness of grooming-awareness training in reducing victimization is **Disputed/insufficiently evaluated**.

---

## 1. What grooming is

Grooming is the deliberate process of building trust, access, and compliance in order to sexually abuse a child and to keep the abuse secret. It is not a single act, a compliment, or a "bad feeling." It is a **pattern** that typically involves:

- selecting a child and assessing the child's environment,
- gaining access through a role, relationship, or institution,
- building trust with the child **and** with the adults around the child,
- testing boundaries and normalizing sexual content or contact,
- isolating the child emotionally or physically,
- maintaining silence through shame, secrecy, threats, or apparent affection.

The Australian National Office for Child Safety describes the process as targeting, trust-building, and isolation, and notes that stages may occur in any order and need not all occur. That caveat is important: **grooming is not a checklist**. Offenders adapt to the child, the family, and the setting.

**Grooming is also done to adults and institutions.** "Institutional grooming" or "organizational grooming" describes an offender building a reputation as trustworthy, indispensable, and beyond suspicion, so that concerns about them are dismissed. This is why safeguarding must target **roles and systems**, not only stranger danger.

## 2. The five-stage model

Winters et al. (2020) reviewed the literature and identified 77 grooming behaviors within five overarching stages:

1. **Victim selection:** identifying a child perceived as accessible and less likely to disclose.
2. **Gaining access and isolating the child:** securing a role or relationship that permits one-to-one contact, then separating the child from protective relationships.
3. **Trust development:** becoming the child's confidant, benefactor, and "safe" adult; grooming the family simultaneously.
4. **Desensitization to sexual content and physical contact:** gradually introducing sexual language, jokes, images, or touch, and normalizing it.
5. **Maintenance following abuse:** sustaining silence and continued access through secrecy, guilt, threats, gifts, or the appearance of a loving relationship.

This model is descriptive and useful for training and forensic interviewing. It is **not** a validated sequential theory: offenders skip stages, loop back, run multiple targets in parallel, and may never progress to contact abuse.

An earlier online typology from O'Connell (2003), based on posing as a minor in chat rooms, identified six stages: friendship-forming, relationship-forming, risk assessment, exclusivity, sexual, and concluding (damage limitation or "hit-and-run"). Elliott's (2017) self-regulation model describes a potentiality phase (rapport-building, incentivization, disinhibition, security management) followed by a disclosure phase in which strategies adjust to the victim's responses.

**Synthesis:** the stage models agree on the functions (access, trust, testing, isolation, secrecy) even when they disagree on labels and order.

## 3. How grooming actually looks

### 3.1 With the child

Common behaviors reported by survivors and offenders:

- **Special attention and flattery:** making the child feel uniquely valued, "mature," or understood.
- **Gifts, money, treats, and privileges:** creating obligation and secrecy; sometimes filling a material need the family cannot meet.
- **Shared secrets:** "this is just between us," "your parents wouldn't understand."
- **Boundary erosion:** tickling, wrestling, lap-sitting, massages, sharing a bed, nudity, bathroom "accidents," increasingly sexual jokes.
- **Testing disclosure:** asking about the child's friends, what the child tells their parents, whether the child has been abused before.
- **Isolation:** encouraging distance from friends, criticizing the family, arranging private trips, creating a "we vs. them" dynamic.
- **Role capture:** becoming the child's mentor, coach, tutor, confidant, or surrogate parent.
- **Desensitization:** introducing sexual content gradually, first as jokes or "education," then images, then contact.
- **Coercion and threats:** threats to withdraw love, expose the child, harm the family, or share images.
- **Apparent romance:** with adolescents, presenting the relationship as love, which increases compliance and delays disclosure.

In a survey of survivors using the Sexual Grooming Scale–Victim Version, 99% endorsed at least one grooming behavior, with an average of 14.25 behaviors reported per survivor (Winters et al., 2024). The most frequently reported behaviors were:

| Behavior | Survivors reporting |
|---|---:|
| Offender presented as nice/charming/likeable | 70% |
| Selected a child who was compliant/trusting | 68% |
| Selected a child with low self-esteem | 61% |
| Arranged activities alone with the child | 57% |
| Showed large amounts of attention | 56% |
| Showed large amounts of affection | 54% |
| Used seemingly innocent touch | 51% |

These are retrospective reports from a help-seeking sample; they describe the **phenomenology** of grooming, not its base rate in the population. Still, the pattern is clear: **the grooming relationship looks like a very good relationship.**

### 3.2 With the family and community

- Becoming the "helpful" adult: babysitting, driving, tutoring, fixing things, offering respite.
- Befriending parents, especially a stressed or isolated parent.
- Choosing families where supervision is stretched, conflict is high, or a parent is absent or ill.
- Cultivating a reputation for integrity, generosity, and devotion to children.
- Using professional or religious authority to discourage questions.
- Isolating the family from extended relatives or community who might notice.

A 2025 dissertation study of residential settings found that in 54.1% of cases the offender was selected because the guardian trusted the offender; 70.9% of offenders said they did not know what made the victim appear vulnerable. The finding is **Emerging** (single study, dissertation), but it fits the larger literature: **trust is the access mechanism, and guardians are part of the target set.**

### 3.3 With institutions

Institutional grooming includes:

- volunteering for roles with unsupervised access,
- resisting or circumventing safeguarding policies,
- cultivating supervisors and colleagues,
- choosing settings with weak reporting cultures,
- using "boundary-pushing" to test whether anyone intervenes.

The Royal Commission and subsequent safeguarding research emphasize that organizational culture, not just individual screening, determines whether grooming succeeds. See [`06-prevention.md`](06-prevention.md).

## 4. Online grooming

### 4.1 What is different online

Online grooming compresses time, widens the pool of potential victims, and removes the physical presence that might alert adults. It also produces a digital trail that can support detection and prosecution, if it is preserved and reported.

A scoping review of 93 papers found that pre-internet grooming strategies included enticements, coercion, isolation, substance abuse, gradual sexualization, and secrecy, while post-internet strategies added risk assessment, fantasy, media progression, meeting, deception, and repetition (Child Abuse & Neglect, 2022). The core functions did not change; the **speed, scale, and evidence trail** did.

### 4.2 Common online tactics

- **Platform selection:** games, social media, chat apps, forums, and livestreaming sites where children gather.
- **Age and identity deception:** usually *not* pretending to be a child. In the N-JOV study, only 5% of offenders pretended to be teens (Wolak et al., 2008). Most victims knew they were talking to an older person.
- **Rapport and "love bombing":** intense attention, compliments, shared interests, emotional intimacy.
- **Sexualization:** gradual introduction of sexual talk, requests for images, or "truth or dare" games.
- **Desensitization to images:** sending adult pornography or CSAM, normalizing sexual content.
- **Secrecy and isolation:** moving to encrypted or disappearing-message apps, asking the child to delete chats, discouraging them from telling parents.
- **Risk assessment:** asking whether the child's parents check their phone, whether they have been abused before, who they trust.
- **Meeting:** arranging in-person contact, sometimes with transportation, gifts, or threats.
- **Financial sextortion:** obtaining an intimate image, then demanding money under threat of exposure. This has become a major and growing category.

### 4.3 Prevalence of online victimization

- A 2025 *Lancet Child & Adolescent Health* systematic review and meta-analysis (86 records, 123 studies) estimated past-year prevalence of online child sexual exploitation and abuse at **8.1%** overall, with online solicitation at **12.5%**, non-consensual taking/sharing/exposure to sexual images at **12.6%**, online sexual exploitation at **4.7%**, and sexual extortion at **3.5%** (Fry et al., 2025).
- An earlier *Journal of Adolescent Health* meta-analysis found mean prevalence of unwanted online sexual exposure at **20.3%** and online sexual solicitation at **11.5%** (2018).
- A U.S. national survey estimated lifetime online grooming by adults at **5.4%**, online child sexual abuse at **15.6%**, image-based sexual abuse at **11.0%**, and sextortion at **3.5%** (NIJ, 2022).
- A 2023 UNH Crimes Against Children Research Center survey found that **one in six** respondents had experienced online child sexual abuse before 18; about **30%** of perpetrators were other juveniles, and about a third of victims did not know the offender's identity (UNH CCRC, 2023).

These estimates vary widely because definitions and samples vary. The consistent finding is that online victimization is **common, often peer-involved, and frequently not reported**.

### 4.4 Sextortion

Financial sextortion is a distinct and fast-growing pattern:

- NCMEC received more than **50,000** reports of financially motivated sextortion in 2025, averaging **137 per day**, up from more than 36,000 in 2024 (NCMEC, 2026).
- In 2024, NCMEC received nearly 100 financial sextortion reports per day; it was aware of more than **three dozen teenage boys** who died by suicide after being victimized (NCMEC, 2024).
- Most public reports come from the child victim or a parent (94% when the relationship is known), which suggests that shame and fear suppress reporting to platforms and police (NCMEC, 2026).

Sextortion is not a "grooming" relationship in the traditional sense; it can be a one-session crime. But it uses the same levers: shame, secrecy, threat, and the child's fear of adult consequences.

## 5. Warning signs

Warning signs are probabilistic. None proves abuse; none should be treated as a diagnosis. They are reasons to **ask, observe, and consult**, not to accuse.

### 5.1 In an adult's behavior toward a child

- Unusual interest in a child's body, development, or sexual knowledge.
- Repeatedly engineering one-to-one time, especially overnight or isolated settings.
- Giving gifts, money, or privileges without a clear reason, and asking for secrecy.
- Boundary violations: tickling, wrestling, lap-sitting, massages, sharing a bed, changing in front of the child.
- Excessive contact with a child via phone, social media, gaming, or messaging, especially late at night.
- Discouraging the child from spending time with friends or family.
- Reacting defensively, angrily, or litigiously to ordinary safeguarding questions.
- Treating a child as a peer or romantic partner, or describing the child as "mature for their age."
- Refusing to follow organizational boundary policies, or seeking settings with weak oversight.

### 5.2 In a child's behavior

- Sudden withdrawal, regression, anxiety, or aggression.
- Sleep disturbances, nightmares, or new fears of a place or person.
- Age-inappropriate sexual knowledge, language, or behavior.
- Avoidance of a specific adult, place, or activity.
- Unexplained gifts, money, or devices.
- Secrecy about online contacts, or distress after device use.
- Self-harm, substance use, or suicidal ideation.
- Physical signs: pain, bleeding, discharge, bruising in genital or anal areas (requires medical assessment).
- A sudden change in a previously close relationship with an adult: either clinging or avoidance.

### 5.3 In an online context

- An older "friend" the child is secretive about.
- Requests for images, video calls, or meetings.
- Pressure to move to a private or encrypted app.
- Gifts, game currency, or money from an online contact.
- Threats or blackmail.
- The child deleting messages or hiding screens.

The Australian National Office for Child Safety's list of grooming signs includes an unusually close connection with an older person, unexplained gifts or money, secrecy, and having an older "boyfriend/girlfriend." These are useful conversation starters, not evidence.

## 6. Why "nice" is not a safety signal

The most important correction this repository can make is that **grooming is designed to look like good character**. The offender is often the most helpful, patient, generous adult in the child's life. That is not a coincidence; it is the method.

Implications:

1. **Trust your discomfort, not their reputation.** Reputation is part of the grooming toolkit.
2. **Audit behavior, not personality.** Ask: who has unsupervised one-to-one access? Who resists boundaries? Who is unusually interested in a particular child?
3. **Do not rely on "stranger danger."** Most abuse is by known persons. Stranger-focused messaging can make children less likely to disclose abuse by a trusted adult, because they have been taught that danger looks like a stranger.
4. **Do not rely on children to stop it.** Children are taught obedience to adults and are often emotionally bound to the offender. Prevention is an adult responsibility.

## 7. Disclosure: what to expect and how to respond

Most victims delay or never disclose. A U.S. national sample found that 66.3% of episodes among 10–17-year-olds were not reported to a parent or any adult, and police reports occurred in only 19.1% of cases (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019). A U.K. review found that over 70% of victims do not disclose within five years, about 1 in 5 never disclose, and the average delay among those who do come forward is around 20 years (CHILD USA, 2024, summarizing multiple studies).

Why:

- fear of not being believed,
- fear of consequences for the offender or family,
- shame and self-blame,
- affection or loyalty toward the offender,
- threats,
- lack of vocabulary,
- prior negative responses to disclosure.

**How to respond to a disclosure:**

1. **Stay calm.** Your reaction becomes the child's memory of telling.
2. **Believe and thank them.** "Thank you for telling me. I believe you. This is not your fault."
3. **Do not interrogate.** Ask only what is needed for safety; detailed questioning can contaminate a forensic interview.
4. **Do not promise secrecy.** Explain that you will need to tell someone who can help keep them safe.
5. **Ensure immediate safety** and contact the appropriate authority or hotline.
6. **Get the child professional support**, and get support for yourself.

In the United States: **Childhelp 1-800-422-4453**, **NCMEC 1-800-THE-LOST**, **RAINN 800-656-4673**, or **911** for immediate danger. Outside the U.S., contact the local child protection service or police.

## 8. What the evidence says about grooming-awareness education

- School-based CSA prevention programs reliably improve **knowledge** and **self-protection skills** (Lu et al., 2023; Gubbels et al., 2021).
- Evidence that these programs reduce **actual victimization** is much weaker. A 2025 INSPIRE systematic review of reviews rated school-based sexual abuse awareness programs as "not currently supported by evidence" due to insufficient or mixed evidence of success across primary studies.
- Programs with more than three sessions and children aged 8+ tend to show larger knowledge effects (Lu et al., 2023).
- Bystander and community-level interventions are promising but have mixed evidence; the same INSPIRE review rated bystander interventions as not currently supported by sufficient evidence.

**The honest conclusion:** teaching children about grooming is worthwhile, but it is not a substitute for adult supervision, organizational safeguards, and environmental design. The burden of prevention belongs to adults and institutions.
