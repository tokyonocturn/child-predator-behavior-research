# Prevention: Protective Factors, Safeguarding, and Evidence

**Evidence grade summary:** Situational prevention and organizational safeguarding are **Established in rationale and promising in practice**. School-based programs reliably improve knowledge and self-protection skills (**Established**), but evidence for reduced victimization is **Disputed/Insufficient**. Bystander programs are **Emerging/Mixed**. Offender registries and residence restrictions are **Disputed** as prevention tools. Digital safety and disclosure response rest on **Emerging** evidence and strong practice consensus.

---

## 1. The prevention frame: public health, not witch-hunting

CSA prevention has historically oscillated between two failed poles:

- **Stranger danger:** teaching children to fear unknown adults, which addresses a minority of cases and can inhibit disclosure about known offenders.
- **Offender registries and residence restrictions:** public lists that may satisfy accountability goals but have not been shown to reduce first-time or repeat offending, and can increase instability and isolation for people under supervision.

The public health approach is different. It identifies **risk and protective factors** at individual, relationship, community, and societal levels; designs interventions at each level; and evaluates outcomes. CDC's socio-ecological model is the standard framework, and it is the one used here.

The core insight is that **opportunity is preventable even when motivation is not**. Finkelhor's preconditions model and situational crime prevention both imply that reducing access, isolation, and guardianship failure can prevent abuse without needing to identify offenders in advance.

## 2. Protective factors

Protective factors are conditions associated with lower risk or better outcomes. They are not guarantees, and they operate alongside risk rather than canceling it.

### 2.1 Child-level

- **A trusted, responsive adult.** The single most consistent protective relationship in the literature. Children who have at least one adult who listens and believes them are more likely to disclose and less likely to be isolated.
- **Knowledge of body autonomy and naming.** Children who can name body parts, recognize unsafe touch, and say no are better positioned to resist and report, though knowledge alone does not prevent abuse by a trusted adult.
- **Social connection.** Friends, activities, and belonging reduce the isolation that offenders exploit.
- **Emotional regulation and assertiveness.** Useful, but not a substitute for adult protection; do not turn protection into a child's job.

### 2.2 Family-level

- **Supervision and knowledge of caregivers.** Knowing who is with your child, where, and under what conditions. This is the most practical protective factor available to parents.
- **Open communication and calm responses to small disclosures.** Children test whether telling is safe by disclosing small things first.
- **Clear privacy and boundary rules.** Closed doors, bathing, sleeping arrangements, device rules, and no secrets about bodies.
- **Reduced isolation.** Connection to extended family, friends, school, and community; avoiding situations where one adult is the sole gateway to the child's social world.
- **Parental monitoring online.** Not surveillance for its own sake, but knowing platforms, contacts, and patterns, and being approachable when something goes wrong.
- **Modeling consent and respect.** Children learn boundaries by experiencing them.

### 2.3 Community and institutional

- **Capable guardianship:** adults who are present, observant, and willing to intervene.
- **Organizational safeguards:** codes of conduct, two-adult rules, open visibility, screening, training, reporting channels, and a culture that takes concerns seriously.
- **Safe, stable, nurturing relationships and environments:** CDC's overarching prevention frame.
- **Economic and social support for families:** reducing stress, isolation, and instability that erode supervision.
- **School connectedness:** a consistent protective factor for adolescent risk behavior, including victimization.

### 2.4 What is not a protective factor

- A father's body weight (see [`04-father-protection-theory.md`](04-father-protection-theory.md)).
- A family's income, race, religion, or structure, treated as a proxy for safety.
- A child's obedience, shyness, or "good behavior."
- A person's reputation for being good with children.
- A criminal background check alone. Checks catch some prior convictions; they do not predict first offenses.

## 3. Situational prevention: the strongest organizational approach

Situational crime prevention (SCP) focuses on the immediate environment in which abuse occurs: who has access, where contact happens, how visible it is, and whether adults feel responsible for intervening. It does not require identifying offenders in advance.

### 3.1 The logic

For abuse to occur, there must be:

1. a person with the potential to offend,
2. a child in a vulnerable position,
3. an environment that enables access and privacy,
4. an opportunity.

SCP modifies 3 and 4. It increases the effort, increases the risk of detection, reduces the reward, and removes excuses for inaction.

### 3.2 Practical measures

| Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Increase effort** | Two-adult rule; no unobserved one-to-one contact; locked or windowed doors; sign-in systems; supervision ratios |
| **Increase risk of detection** | Open visibility in classrooms, locker rooms, and coaching spaces; random spot checks; parents present at activities; staff walk-throughs |
| **Reduce reward** | No overnight trips with a single adult; no private messaging with minors; no gifts or money; no special one-child privileges |
| **Remove excuses** | Clear codes of conduct; signed boundary policies; mandatory reporting procedures; "it is always okay to interrupt" norms |
| **Reduce provocation/trigger** | Alcohol-free events; structured activities; avoiding isolated late-night settings |
| **Control facilitators** | Device and camera policies; no private social media contact with minors; visitor management |

### 3.3 Evidence

- The Australian Royal Commission and subsequent reviews recommend situational prevention as the core organizational strategy for CSA (Kaufman et al., 2019).
- NIJ (2024) describes situational prevention as "limited but promising," with structural and policy strategies showing potential to reduce perpetration in youth-serving organizations.
- A crime-script analysis of CSA in youth-serving organizations shows how offender behavior unfolds across stages and identifies intervention points (Leclerc, 2026).
- A national U.S. survey found that 3.75% of adults experienced CSA in a youth-serving organization, and that younger cohorts reported a lower proportion of abuse in major national youth organizations than older cohorts (29.1% vs. 44.5%), consistent with prevention efforts having some effect (Assini-Meytin et al., 2025). This is **Emerging** evidence, not proof of causation.

### 3.4 What organizations should have

At minimum:

1. A written child protection policy, reviewed annually.
2. A code of conduct with explicit boundary rules.
3. Screening and reference checks, with the understanding that they are imperfect.
4. Supervision ratios and a two-adult rule for all activities.
5. Open, visible physical spaces; no isolated one-to-one settings.
6. A no-private-messaging rule between adults and minors.
7. A clear, accessible reporting pathway, including for concerns about staff.
8. Mandatory reporting training and a designated safeguarding lead.
9. A culture that responds to boundary violations before abuse occurs.
10. Regular audits, incident reviews, and feedback from children and parents.

These are not guarantees. They raise the cost of offending and increase the chance of early detection.

## 4. School-based prevention programs

### 4.1 What works

Meta-analyses consistently find that school-based CSA prevention programs improve **knowledge** and **self-protection skills**:

- Lu et al. (2023): 31 studies, 9,049 participants; knowledge effect g = 0.72 (questionnaire) and g = 0.55 (vignette); programs with more than three sessions and children aged 8+ were more effective.
- Gubbels et al. (2021): 34 studies for knowledge and 22 for skills; overall effect d = 0.572 for knowledge and d = 0.528 for self-protection skills; programs with social-emotional skills, self-blame content, puppets, and games showed larger effects.
- A 2026 systematic review of 18 RCTs found improvements in knowledge and attitudes, particularly about consent and awareness, but less frequent and less consistent evidence of behavioral change; short follow-up and attrition limited conclusions.

### 4.2 What is uncertain

- **Behavioral outcomes.** Knowledge gains do not reliably translate into lower victimization. Measuring actual abuse reduction is difficult, and few studies are powered or designed to do it.
- **Long-term retention.** Most evaluations follow children for months, not years.
- **Disclosure effects.** Some programs increase disclosure; whether that reflects more abuse being reported or more false reports is a question that requires careful study. The evidence generally does not support fears of a false-report surge.
- **Age and dosage.** Programs for very young children show smaller effects; more than three sessions are better than one-off assemblies.
- **Cultural fit.** Most evaluations are from high-income Western countries; adaptation matters.

### 4.3 The INSPIRE caution

A 2025 systematic review of reviews updating the WHO INSPIRE framework rated **school-based sexual abuse awareness programs as "not currently supported by evidence"** because of insufficient or mixed evidence of success across primary studies. This does not mean the programs are harmful or useless. It means the evidence that they **reduce violence** is weaker than the evidence that they **increase knowledge**. Honest prevention messaging should say both.

### 4.4 What good programs include

- Correct names for body parts and clear body-safety concepts.
- Recognizing unsafe situations and unsafe secrets.
- Assertiveness and "no" skills.
- Identifying trusted adults and practicing disclosure.
- Repeated, developmentally appropriate sessions (more than three).
- Whole-school culture and parent involvement, not just a classroom lesson.
- Avoidance of victim-blaming and of messages that imply children must prevent their own abuse.

## 5. Bystander and community interventions

Bystander programs teach adults and peers to recognize risky situations and intervene. They are widely used in sexual violence prevention on college campuses and in some youth settings.

**Evidence status:** **Emerging/Mixed.** Some studies show improvements in bystander attitudes and behaviors; the 2025 INSPIRE review rated bystander interventions as not currently supported by sufficient evidence of violence reduction. The logic is sound (most abuse occurs in contexts where other adults could notice), but the evidence base for CSA-specific outcomes is thin.

For families and communities, the practical version is **collective guardianship**: multiple adults who know the child, know each other, and feel responsible for noticing. This is not a program; it is a social norm.

## 6. Digital safety and online prevention

### 6.1 For parents and caregivers

- **Know the platforms.** Games, chat apps, and social media all have private messaging. Ask which ones your child uses and how they work.
- **Set privacy settings together.** Defaults are not safe defaults.
- **Establish device rules.** Charging outside bedrooms, no devices in bathrooms, age-appropriate time limits, and a family agreement about what is shared.
- **Talk about images.** Once an image is sent, it cannot be recalled. No adult or older peer needs a picture of a child's body.
- **Normalize coming to you.** The most important protection is that a child will tell you about a scary or embarrassing online interaction without fear of punishment.
- **Do not ban and disappear.** A child who loses their device for disclosing will not disclose again. Respond to the offender, not the child.

### 6.2 For children and teens

- Do not send images to anyone you have not met in person, and be cautious even then.
- An adult who asks for secrecy, gifts, or images is not a friend.
- If someone threatens you, **do not pay, do not send more, and tell an adult immediately**. Preserve the messages and report.
- You can always come to a parent or trusted adult, even if you broke a rule.

### 6.3 Sextortion response

Financial sextortion is a crime, and the victim is not at fault. Practical guidance consistent with FBI and NCMEC messaging:

1. **Stop all contact.** Do not negotiate or pay; payment often increases demands.
2. **Preserve evidence.** Screenshots, usernames, URLs, and payment requests.
3. **Report** to the platform, NCMEC's CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org), and local police.
4. **Get support.** Sextortion is associated with severe shame and suicidal ideation, particularly among boys. Immediate mental health support matters.
5. **Do not delete the account** before preserving evidence; deactivation may be appropriate after reporting.

### 6.4 Platform and policy measures

- Age assurance and safer defaults for minors.
- Detection and reporting of grooming and CSAM.
- Rapid preservation and law-enforcement referral.
- Design changes that reduce unsolicited contact from adults.
- Transparency reporting on enticement and sextortion.

These are policy levers, not individual safeguards. They matter, but they are outside a parent's direct control.

## 7. Responding to disclosure and suspicion

### 7.1 If a child discloses

1. **Stay calm and listen.** Do not show shock, anger, or disgust.
2. **Believe and reassure.** "Thank you for telling me. I believe you. It is not your fault."
3. **Do not interrogate.** Ask only what you need to know for immediate safety.
4. **Do not promise secrecy.** Explain that you will tell someone who can help.
5. **Ensure safety.** Contact the appropriate authority or hotline.
6. **Get support** for the child and for yourself.

### 7.2 If you suspect an adult

- Focus on behavior, not personality: isolation, boundary violations, secrecy, gifts, and special access.
- Do not confront the person alone or investigate yourself.
- Report to the organization's safeguarding lead and, where required, to child protection authorities or police.
- Document what you observed, not what you concluded.
- Do not warn the person or allow them to "explain" before a report is made.

### 7.3 Mandatory reporting

Many jurisdictions require professionals who work with children to report suspected abuse. Requirements vary by country and state. Know your local duty, and remember that a report is a request for an investigation, not an accusation.

**U.S. resources:** Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline **1-800-422-4453**; NCMEC **1-800-THE-LOST**; RAINN **800-656-4673**; **911** for immediate danger.

## 8. Offender-focused prevention

Prevention is not only about protecting children; it is also about reducing offending and reoffending.

- **Treatment.** Cognitive-behavioral and relapse-prevention programs can reduce recidivism for some offenders; effects are modest and depend on risk level, treatment quality, and follow-up.
- **Risk management.** Supervision, stable housing, employment, and social support reduce recidivism; isolation and instability increase it.
- **Registration and residence restrictions.** These are **Disputed** as prevention tools. They may support accountability and investigation, but evidence that they reduce reoffending is limited, and they can increase housing instability and isolation.
- **Prevention for people with pedophilic interests.** Some individuals seek help before offending. Stigma and criminalization of help-seeking can increase risk. Prevention-oriented mental health services for non-offending pedophiles are an **Emerging** area with a plausible public-health rationale, though evidence on effectiveness is limited.
- **Juvenile intervention.** Developmentally appropriate treatment and safeguarding for juveniles with harmful sexual behavior, rather than lifelong adult-style registration. Most juvenile offenders do not become adult offenders.

## 9. What the evidence does not support

| Strategy | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Stranger danger" as the primary message | **Unsupported as primary strategy** | Most abuse is by known persons; can inhibit disclosure |
| Father's body weight as a risk screen | **Unsupported** | No evidence; false reassurance; stigma |
| Public registries as a standalone prevention tool | **Disputed** | Limited evidence of recidivism reduction; collateral harms |
| One-off school assemblies | **Insufficient** | Dose matters; more than three sessions better |
| Teaching children to resist without adult safeguards | **Insufficient** | Shifts responsibility to children; ignores power imbalance |
| Polygraph-based screening | **Disputed** | Weak validity; not a substitute for supervision |
| "Just trust your gut" | **Insufficient alone** | Intuition can be useful but is shaped by bias and reputation |

## 10. A practical prevention checklist

### For families

- [ ] I know every adult who has unsupervised one-to-one access to my child.
- [ ] My child knows they can tell me anything, even if they broke a rule.
- [ ] We have clear rules about privacy, bodies, devices, and overnight stays.
- [ ] I know which platforms my child uses and who they talk to.
- [ ] I have met the adults in my child's activities, and I drop in unannounced sometimes.
- [ ] I model consent and respect, and I do not keep secrets about bodies.
- [ ] I know how to report a concern and what my local mandatory reporting rules are.

### For organizations

- [ ] Written child protection policy, reviewed annually.
- [ ] Code of conduct with boundary rules.
- [ ] Two-adult rule and open visibility.
- [ ] No private messaging between adults and minors.
- [ ] Screening, references, and induction training.
- [ ] Accessible reporting pathway and designated lead.
- [ ] Regular audits and incident reviews.
- [ ] Children and parents know how to raise a concern.

### For communities

- [ ] Support for single parents, stepfamilies, and families under stress.
- [ ] Safe, stable, nurturing environments for children.
- [ ] School connectedness and youth programs.
- [ ] Public awareness that most abuse is by known persons.
- [ ] Accessible mental health and survivor services.
- [ ] Policies that hold institutions accountable, not just individuals.
