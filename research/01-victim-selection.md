# Victim Selection: Who Is Targeted, and Why

**Evidence grade summary:** Offender–victim relationship patterns and age/gender distributions are **Established**. Specific family-structure effects are **Established as associations** but **Disputed** as causal explanations. Offender self-reports about "selection criteria" are **Emerging**, because samples are small, convicted, and self-presentational.

---

## 1. The central finding: selection is about access and perceived disclosure risk

The most consistent finding in the literature is not that offenders search for a particular *type* of child, but that they search for a child they can **access**, **isolate**, and **keep silent**. NCMEC's behavioral-analysis monograph puts it directly: people who molest children "often seek legitimate access to children and then victimize them through a process similar to seduction" (Lanning, 2010). Access can be familial, occupational, recreational, digital, or residential.

This reframing matters for prevention. A checklist of child traits will always fail, because the common factor is the offender's relationship to the child's environment, not the child's personality. What caregivers can change is the environment: supervision, privacy, one-adult-one-child situations, and the ease with which an adult can become indispensable.

**Established:** In the large majority of cases, the offender is known to the child or family. CDC estimates about 90% of CSA is perpetrated by someone known and trusted by the child or family (CDC, 2024). National victimization data and national prevalence surveys converge on this pattern.

---

## 2. Age patterns

CSA is not confined to young children. In a nationally representative U.S. sample of 13,052 children and adolescents, victimization was concentrated in adolescence, particularly ages 14–17, and most offenses were committed by other juveniles (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019). In an Australian offender sample, the mean age at first abuse incident was 10.45 years, with 11% under 5, 42% aged 6–11, and 47% over 12 (Crime Science, 2015).

Two implications follow:

1. **Adolescents are the largest victim group in community samples.** Prevention messaging that focuses only on young children misses most of the population burden.
2. **Age patterns depend on the data source.** Official child-protective-services data skew younger; self-report and crime-victimization surveys skew adolescent. Neither is "the" true distribution.

The Australian Child Maltreatment Study (n = 8,503) found a national CSA prevalence of 28.5%, with substantial prevalence across perpetrator classes, including other known adolescents (10.0%), parents/caregivers in the home (7.8%), other known adults (7.5%), and unknown adults (4.9%) (Mathews et al., 2024).

**Do not conclude** that younger children are "safer." Age-specific risk depends on definition, setting, and reporting. Infants and very young children are abused, and non-contact and familial abuse of young children is widely believed to be under-detected.

---

## 3. Gender patterns

- **Girls** are more frequently identified as victims in official and self-report data. Stoltenborgh et al. (2011) estimated self-reported CSA at 180 per 1,000 for females and 76 per 1,000 for males.
- **Boys** are substantially under-identified relative to self-report. Male victimization is more likely to involve male offenders, and male victims are less likely to disclose in some studies.
- In the Gewirtz-Meydan and Finkelhor (2019) sample, girls were mostly abused by males (88.4%), while boys were abused by both males (45.6%) and females (54.4%).
- **Gender is not a protective factor.** It changes the probable context and the disclosure pathway, not the possibility of harm.

A caution on interpretation: differences in identified prevalence reflect a mix of true incidence, willingness to disclose, definitional choices (e.g., whether peer sexual assault counts), and institutional reporting. They are not evidence that one sex is inherently more "targetable."

---

## 4. Family structure and household composition

### What the evidence shows

Several large reviews and surveillance systems find associations between household composition and CSA risk:

- Living with one parent rather than two is associated with elevated risk in some studies (AASAS, 2014, reviewing the literature).
- The presence of a stepfather in the home has been associated with roughly double the risk of victimization for girls in some analyses (AASAS, 2014).
- Not living with one's natural parents for extended periods is associated with increased risk of victimization by non-biological family members (AASAS, 2014).
- CDC lists "caregivers in the home who are not a biological parent" as a risk factor for child abuse and neglect perpetration, in the context of general maltreatment rather than CSA specifically (CDC, 2024).

These findings are real associations. They are also easy to misread.

### What the evidence does not show

1. **They do not show that single-parent or stepfamilies cause abuse.** Household composition is confounded with income, housing instability, parental stress, social isolation, child-care arrangements, and the number of unrelated adults passing through the home. Studies rarely isolate the household variable from these.
2. **They do not identify a "type" of child who is targeted.** The mechanism is more plausibly about *access and guardianship*: a non-biological adult in the home has both opportunity and, in some cases, weaker long-term investment in the child's protection. That is a statement about the adult's position, not the child's worth or the mother's competence.
3. **They do not support stigmatizing single mothers.** Most single-parent households are safe. Most stepfathers do not abuse. A risk factor with a modest effect size is not a prediction about a household.
4. **Two-parent households are not protective by definition.** In the Australian offender sample above, 53% of first victims were familial; in a small Turkish clinical sample, 52.1% of victims lived with both parents (Hatay study, 2016). Abuse occurs inside intact families, and fathers are themselves perpetrators in a meaningful share of cases.

### A note on "father absence"

"Father absence" is often invoked in popular accounts as a master explanation. The peer-reviewed literature treats it as one correlate among many, with weak causal identification. A 2005 review of fathers and physical abuse risk noted that the pathway from father absence to maltreatment is not well understood and may run through poverty and family stress rather than through father absence itself (Guterman & Lee, 2005). That review concerns physical abuse, not CSA, and is cited here only to show how cautiously the "absent father" literature is treated by researchers.

**Bottom line:** household composition is a marker of structural vulnerability and access, not a diagnosis of a family. Prevention should target the conditions (isolation, unsupervised access, economic stress), not the family form.

## 5. Disability, isolation, and prior victimization

Three child-level factors recur across studies:

- **Disability.** Children with disabilities are at elevated risk of abuse, including sexual abuse, in multiple reviews. CDC lists special needs that increase caregiver burden as a risk factor for maltreatment. The mechanism is likely a combination of increased caregiver contact, reduced communication, institutional settings, and offenders' perception of lower disclosure risk. This is an **Established** association; the precise magnitude for CSA specifically is **Disputed** because definitions and samples vary.
- **Social isolation.** Children who are lonely, have few friends, or lack a trusted adult are described by offenders as preferred targets. In a survey of survivors, 68% reported that the offender selected a child who was compliant/trusting and 61% a child with low self-esteem (Winters et al., 2024). These are retrospective survivor reports, not prospective predictors; they describe what offenders exploited, not what causes victimization.
- **Prior victimization.** Offenders report perceiving previously abused children as easier to abuse and less likely to be believed. In one small sample of 16 offenders, all said they were more likely to target a child who had prior sexual contact and was perceived to know a lot about sex (AIC, 2015). This is a small, convicted, qualitative sample and should be read as **Emerging**. It also cuts against the idea that "the system protects children who have already disclosed": prior victimization can increase rather than reduce risk.

**Important:** these factors describe *vulnerability to exploitation*, not *responsibility for it*. An offender's perception that a child is "easy" is a fact about the offender's decision-making, not about the child's character.

## 6. Offender–victim relationships

Relationship data are among the most robust findings in the field, though the exact percentages vary by data source.

### U.S. national victimization data

In the National Survey of Children's Exposure to Violence (n = 13,052), most offenses were at the hands of other juveniles (70.1% for female victims and 76.7% for male victims), primarily acquaintances (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019). This is a broader definition of "sexual abuse and assault" than some clinical studies use, and it includes peer offending that many legal definitions treat separately.

### U.K. national data (contact CSA, adults reporting childhood abuse)

Office for National Statistics data, reproduced in the CSA Centre's evidence review, show the following distribution of perpetrator relationship for contact CSA before age 16 (ONS, 2020, Table 12b):

| Perpetrator | All adults |
|---|---:|
| Friend or acquaintance | 37.7% |
| Other family member | 23.4% |
| Stranger | 21.0% |
| Person in position of trust or authority | 9.7% |
| Partner or previous partner | 9.3% |
| Stepfather | 6.3% |
| Father | 5.4% |
| Mother | 0.8% |
| Stepmother | 0.2% |
| Carer/guardian | 0.3% |

Percentages sum to more than 100 because respondents could report multiple perpetrators. The headline: **acquaintances and family members together account for the majority; strangers are a minority**, though a substantial one (about one in five in this survey).

### Australian national data

The Australian Child Maltreatment Study found a 28.5% lifetime CSA prevalence, with perpetrator classes as follows (Mathews et al., 2024):

| Perpetrator class | Prevalence |
|---|---:|
| Other known adolescents (non-romantic) | 10.0% |
| Parents/caregivers in the home | 7.8% |
| Other known adults | 7.5% |
| Unknown adults | 4.9% |
| Adolescents (current/former romantic partners) | 2.5% |
| Institutional caregivers | 2.0% |
| Siblings | 1.6% |
| Unknown adolescents | 1.4% |

### Small clinical and offender samples

- A Turkish clinical sample of 48 offenders found 91.7% knew their victims (Hatay study, 2016). Small and non-representative, but consistent with the larger pattern.
- In an Australian sample of incarcerated offenders who offended in youth-serving organizations, 53% reported a familial first victim and 47% a non-familial first victim, including 6% stranger-onset; 66% of those with a prior relationship had known the victim for more than a year (Crime Science, 2015).

**Synthesis:** the "known vs. stranger" binary is less useful than a continuum of **access roles**: family, family-adjacent, institutional, recreational, digital, and stranger. Prevention should map the access roles in a child's life, not rank strangers against relatives.

## 7. What offenders say they look for, and why to read it carefully

Offender self-report studies produce a recurring list: loneliness, low self-esteem, need for attention, few friends, a chaotic or distracted family, prior abuse, and poor supervision. For example:

- A practitioner review based on interviews with offenders lists loneliness, quiet/passive temperament, attention-seeking, no siblings at home, prior abuse, family dysfunction, and perceived questionable credibility (Police1, 2008, practitioner commentary, not a study).
- The AIC (2015) sample of 16 offenders said they targeted children perceived to know about sex and to have had prior sexual contact; 37.5% said the child was well supervised and 37.5% said the child was never alone. That last finding is important: **supervision alone did not deter this sample**; perceived sexual precocity and access did.

Why the caution:

1. **Small, convicted, self-selected samples.** Sixteen offenders are not a population.
2. **Impression management.** Offenders may present themselves as strategic and discerning, or may retroactively construct a coherent "selection" story.
3. **Survivorship bias in the questions.** Researchers ask offenders who were caught about the children they abused, not about the children they approached and failed with.
4. **Victim-blaming risk.** A list of child traits can slide into "that child was asking for it." The correct reading is: *offenders exploit unmet needs and weak guardianship*. The remedy is meeting needs and strengthening guardianship, not changing children.

In a 2025 dissertation study of residential settings, 70.9% of offenders said they "did not know" what made the victim appear vulnerable; among those who did, age (9.1%), lack of supervision (9.1%), and the victim's trust (5.5%) were most cited (CUNY, 2025). The same study found that in 54.1% of cases the offender was selected because the guardian trusted the offender. That is an **Emerging** finding from a single dissertation, but it points in the same direction as the larger literature: **guardian trust is the access mechanism**.

## 8. Settings and locations

Contact abuse occurs most often in private residential settings (the child's home, the offender's home, or another residence) rather than in public spaces. Lanning (2010) notes that offenders tend to offend in their own home or close to it, and the Swedish modus-operandi research reached the same conclusion for non-familial offenders (Ebberline & Holmberg, 2008). Youth-serving organizations, schools, sports, religious institutions, and residential care are also documented settings; a national U.S. survey estimated that 3.75% of adults experienced CSA in a youth-serving organization (Assini-Meytin et al., 2025).

Online-facilitated abuse has no physical location, which is precisely why place-based prevention is insufficient. See [`03-grooming.md`](03-grooming.md) and [`05-statistics.md`](05-statistics.md).

## 9. What this means for prevention

1. **Map access, not stereotypes.** Identify every adult with unsupervised, one-to-one access to a child, including relatives, family friends, coaches, tutors, clergy, and online contacts.
2. **Reduce isolation.** The most repeated offender-reported vulnerability is a child without a trusted adult or peer group. Isolation is also a *consequence* of grooming, so reducing it serves both prevention and detection.
3. **Treat guardian trust as a control point.** Background checks, references, boundary policies, and surprise presence matter more than trying to spot a "bad person."
4. **Do not use family structure as a risk label.** Support single parents, stepfamilies, and families under economic stress with respite, supervision resources, and community connection rather than suspicion.
5. **Include adolescents and boys.** Prevention that imagines a small child and a stranger misses most of the epidemiological picture.
6. **Make disclosure safe.** Because most victims delay or never disclose, the goal is not a one-time "tell someone" lesson but a sustained environment in which telling is believed and acted on.
