# Statistics: Prevalence, Reporting, Relationships, and Settings

**Evidence grade summary:** The general magnitude of CSA prevalence and the predominance of known offenders are **Established**. Exact percentages are **Disputed** because definitions, samples, and disclosure methods vary enormously. Online victimization trend figures are **Emerging** and change quickly. Any single number presented without its definition and method should be treated as a headline, not a fact.

---

## 1. Why the numbers disagree

Before quoting any statistic, ask four questions:

1. **What counts as abuse?** Non-contact exposure, unwanted solicitation, peer assault, statutory rape, and penetrative abuse produce very different prevalence estimates.
2. **Who is asked?** Children, adults recalling childhood, parents, clinicians, police, and child-protective-services records produce different rates.
3. **How is it asked?** Anonymous self-report surveys yield far higher rates than official records. Stoltenborgh et al. (2011) found 127 per 1,000 in self-report studies versus 4 per 1,000 in informant studies, a 30-fold difference driven largely by method.
4. **What is the recall period?** Past-year, lifetime-to-date, and adult retrospective estimates are not comparable.

The result is a literature in which credible estimates range from about 1% to over 20% for girls, depending on the study. That is not a scandal; it is what happens when a hidden, definitionally contested phenomenon is measured in different ways. The correct response is to report ranges and name the method, not to pick the number that fits a narrative.

## 2. Global prevalence

### 2.1 Meta-analyses

| Source | Sample | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Stoltenborgh et al. (2011), *Child Maltreatment* | 217 publications, 331 samples, 9.9 million participants | Self-reported CSA 127 per 1,000 overall; 180 per 1,000 girls; 76 per 1,000 boys; informant studies 4 per 1,000 |
| Pereda et al. (2009), *Clinical Psychology Review* | 65 studies, 22 countries | 19.7% girls; 7.9% boys |
| Barth et al. (2013), *International Journal of Public Health* | 55 studies, 24 countries | 8–31% for girls and 3–17% for boys across four abuse types; 9 in 100 girls and 3 in 100 boys reported forced intercourse |
| *The Lancet* (2025), global analysis | 204 locations, 1990–2023; age-standardised estimates for 2023 | 18.9% of females (95% UI 16.0–25.2) and 14.8% of males (9.5–23.5); wide regional variation (12.2% to 26.8% for females by super-region). The 18.0% and 7.6% figures sometimes attached to this paper are Stoltenborgh et al.'s (2011) |

### 2.2 Agency estimates

- **WHO (2026):** one in 5 women and 1 in 7 men report having been sexually abused as a child.
- **UNICEF (2026):** about 650 million girls and women alive today (1 in 5) were subjected to sexual violence as children; between 410 and 530 million boys and men (around 1 in 7) experienced sexual violence in childhood.
- **CDC (2024, United States):** at least one in 4 girls and one in 20 boys experience CSA. (An earlier CDC report to Congress used 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 13 boys; the difference reflects definition and data source, not a change in the underlying phenomenon.)

**How to read these:** global agency estimates are modeled from heterogeneous national surveys. They are useful for scale and policy, not for precision. The consistent signal across all methods is that **CSA affects a large minority of girls and a substantial minority of boys worldwide**.

### 2.3 National prevalence studies

- **Australia:** the Australian Child Maltreatment Study (n = 8,503) found a lifetime CSA prevalence of **28.5%**, with 78% of those affected experiencing it multiple times (Mathews et al., 2024).
- **United Kingdom:** the CSA Centre's review reports that 7.5% of adults aged 18–74 experienced CSA before 16 in the Crime Survey for England and Wales, with higher rates in self-report studies using broader definitions (CSA Centre, 2021, 2026).
- **United States:** official child-protective-services data identify about 56,575 children as sexually abused each year (RAINN, citing *Child Maltreatment 2020–2024*). This is a count of **substantiated cases**, not true incidence; self-report estimates are far higher.

## 3. Victim demographics

### 3.1 Age

- Community samples show risk rising through childhood and peaking in adolescence. In the U.S. National Survey of Children's Exposure to Violence (n = 13,052), victimization was most frequent among 14–17-year-olds (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019).
- Offender samples show a broader distribution. In an Australian offender sample, mean age at first abuse incident was 10.45 years, with 11% under 5 and 47% over 12 (Crime Science, 2015).
- Official CPS data skew younger; self-report surveys skew adolescent. Neither is the "true" age distribution.

### 3.2 Gender

- Girls are more frequently identified as victims. Stoltenborgh et al. (2011): 180 per 1,000 girls vs. 76 per 1,000 boys (self-report).
- Boys are more likely to be under-identified and less likely to disclose in some studies. In the U.S. national sample, boys were abused by male (45.6%) and female (54.4%) offenders, while girls were overwhelmingly abused by males (88.4%) (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019).
- Gender differences in identified prevalence reflect true incidence, disclosure, definition, and institutional reporting in unknown proportions. Do not treat gender as a protective factor.

### 3.3 Disability and other vulnerabilities

Children with disabilities are at elevated risk of abuse, including sexual abuse, across multiple reviews. CDC lists special needs that increase caregiver burden as a risk factor for maltreatment. The mechanism likely combines increased caregiver contact, reduced communication, institutional settings, and offenders' perception of lower disclosure risk.

Children in residential care, foster care, and juvenile justice settings are also at elevated risk, though data quality varies by jurisdiction. These are **structural** vulnerabilities, not characteristics that justify blame.

## 4. Offender–victim relationships

This is the most important table in the repository for prevention.

### 4.1 U.S. summary

- **About 90%** of CSA is perpetrated by someone known and trusted by the child or family (CDC, 2024).
- In national victimization data, most offenses against both boys and girls are committed by **other juveniles** (76.7% for male victims and 70.1% for female victims), primarily acquaintances (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019).

### 4.4 What the relationship data mean

1. **"Stranger danger" is a minority strategy.** In U.K. national data, strangers account for 21.0% of contact abuse (ONS, 2020, Table 12b, in CSA Centre, 2021); across other surveys the figure runs lower. The majority involve family, acquaintances, peers, and trusted adults.
2. **Peers and juveniles matter.** In community samples, a large share of sexual assault against minors is committed by other minors. Prevention must include healthy relationships, consent, and harmful sexual behavior responses, not only adult-offender messaging.
3. **Family is not automatically safe.** Fathers, stepfathers, siblings, uncles, and cousins appear in the data. A safeguarding plan that exempts family from scrutiny is incomplete.
4. **Institutional access matters.** Coaches, teachers, clergy, carers, and youth leaders appear across datasets; a U.S. national survey estimated 3.75% of adults experienced CSA in a youth-serving organization (Assini-Meytin et al., 2025).

## 5. Settings and locations

Contact abuse occurs most often in **private residential settings**: the child's home, the offender's home, or another residence. Lanning (2010) notes that offenders tend to offend in their own home or close to it; Swedish modus-operandi research reached the same conclusion for non-familial offenders (Ebberline & Holmberg, 2008).

Other documented settings:

- youth-serving organizations (sports, faith, arts, clubs, mentoring),
- schools and childcare,
- residential care and foster placements,
- online platforms and games (for facilitation, grooming, CSAM, and sextortion),
- public spaces (a minority of contact offenses, often involving adolescents and stranger or acquaintance offenders).

The practical implication: **place-based prevention must cover homes and digital spaces, not just public places.** "Stranger in the park" is a narrow slice of the risk surface.

## 6. Reporting and disclosure

### 6.1 How much is reported?

- A U.S. national sample found that **66.3%** of CSA episodes among 10–17-year-olds were not reported to a parent or any adult, and police reports occurred for only **19.1%** of cases (Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor, 2019).
- The Department of Justice has estimated that **86%** of CSA went unreported by victims before adulthood (cited in CHILD USA, 2024).
- One study estimated that only **10–15%** of all CSA is reported to legal authorities (CHILD USA, 2024, summarizing the literature).
- In Australia, lifetime disclosure among CSA survivors was **54.8%**, meaning **45.2% had never disclosed** at the time of the survey; disclosure was more common among women (60.3%) than men (42.2%) (Mathews et al., 2025).

### 6.2 How long does disclosure take?

- Over **70%** of victims do not disclose within five years (CHILD USA, 2024).
- About **1 in 5** never disclose (CHILD USA, 2024).
- Among those who do come forward, the average delay is around **20 years**; in a Boy Scouts of America sample, over half first disclosed after age 50 (CHILD USA, 2024).
- Children who do disclose in childhood often tell siblings or peers first, not adults (CHILD USA, 2024).

### 6.3 Why this matters for interpretation

Official counts are **detection counts**, not incidence. A low count in a jurisdiction may mean less abuse, better prevention, or worse reporting, and the three are not distinguishable from the count alone. It also means that "no report was made" is not evidence that nothing happened, and that a delayed disclosure is consistent with the base rate rather than a sign of fabrication.

### 7.2 Platform reporting

- **NCMEC CyberTipline, 2024:** 20.5 million reports (29.2 million separate incidents after adjusting for bundled reports); 546,000 online enticement reports (+192% vs. 2023); 26,823 child sex trafficking reports (+55%); nearly 100 financial sextortion reports per day; at least 36 teenage boys known to have died by suicide after sextortion (NCMEC, 2024).
- **NCMEC CyberTipline, 2025:** 21.3 million reports; 1,413,347 online enticement reports; 113,530 child sex trafficking reports; more than 53,000 reports escalated as urgent or involving a child in imminent danger (NCMEC, 2025).
- **Financial sextortion, 2025:** more than 50,000 reports, averaging 137 per day, up from more than 36,000 in 2024; 94% of public reports came from the child victim or a parent when the relationship was known (NCMEC, 2026).

**Caution on trend claims:** reporting volumes are affected by changes in law (e.g., the REPORT Act), platform reporting behavior, bundling features, and detection technology. A rise in reports is not a one-to-one rise in offenses. It is a signal of both increased offending and increased detection.

### 7.3 Who commits online-facilitated offenses?

- Most victims know the offender from offline contexts; about 30% of perpetrators in a national survey were other juveniles, and about a third of victims did not know the offender's identity (UNH CCRC, 2023).
- In the N-JOV study, only 5% of offenders pretended to be teens; most victims knew they were talking to an older person, and 73% of those who met face-to-face did so more than once (Wolak et al., 2008).
- Online-meeting offenders were not more likely to use violence or abduction than offenders who knew victims in person; most cases involved statutory rape or non-contact offenses (Wolak et al., 2013).

## 8. Economic and social cost

- CDC estimates the total lifetime economic burden of CSA in the United States in 2015 at **at least $9.3 billion** (CDC, 2024).
- Costs include healthcare, mental health treatment, child welfare, criminal justice, lost productivity, and reduced educational attainment.

Cost estimates are useful for policy arguments but are sensitive to assumptions about attribution, discount rates, and which outcomes count. Treat them as order-of-magnitude figures.

## 9. Special populations and disparities

- **Children with disabilities:** elevated risk across reviews; mechanisms include dependency, communication barriers, institutional settings, and offender perception of lower disclosure risk.
- **Children in care:** elevated risk in residential and foster settings; data quality varies.
- **Indigenous and minority children:** overrepresentation in some child welfare and justice systems reflects structural disadvantage, reporting bias, and systemic discrimination, not group pathology. Use with care and always in context.
- **LGBTQ+ youth:** some studies report elevated rates of sexual victimization, often associated with family rejection, homelessness, and higher exposure to risky environments. The explanation is structural, not identity-based.
- **Boys and men:** under-identified and under-disclosed; prevalence estimates are likely conservative.

A general rule: when a group shows a higher rate, ask first about **exposure, reporting, and structural vulnerability**, not about the group's character.

## 10. A reader's checklist for CSA statistics

When you see a number, ask:

1. **Definition:** contact, non-contact, or both? Does it include peer assault, statutory rape, online solicitation?
2. **Sample:** nationally representative, clinical, convicted, student, or convenience?
3. **Method:** anonymous self-report, interview, official record, or modeled estimate?
4. **Recall period:** past year, lifetime, or adult retrospective?
5. **Denominator:** per 1,000, per 100,000, percentage of respondents, or percentage of cases?
6. **Source date:** is this a stable base rate or a rapidly changing trend?
7. **Funding and interest:** who produced it, and do they benefit from a high or low number?
8. **Confidence interval:** is the estimate precise or is it a range masquerading as a point?

If a headline cannot answer these, it is not yet a finding.
