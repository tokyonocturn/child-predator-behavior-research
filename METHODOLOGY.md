# Research Methodology

## 1. Purpose and scope

This repository answers a bounded set of questions:

1. What characteristics and circumstances are associated with elevated risk of child sexual abuse (CSA) victimization?
2. What motivates and enables people who sexually abuse children?
3. How does grooming work, in person and online, and what are its observable signs?
4. Is the claim that offenders target children whose fathers weigh under 200 pounds supported by evidence?
5. What do prevalence, disclosure, and offender-relationship data actually show?
6. Which prevention strategies have evidence, and which are plausible but unproven?

The review covers contact and non-contact abuse, in-person and technology-facilitated offending, adult and juvenile offenders, and intrafamilial and extrafamilial contexts. It prioritizes sources from 1980–2026, with foundational theory retained where it remains the standard account.

**Out of scope:** clinical diagnosis, forensic risk assessment of named individuals, legal advice, investigation of specific allegations, and any operational detail that would assist offending.

## 2. Source hierarchy

Sources were weighted in the following order:

1. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** in peer-reviewed journals (e.g., *Child Abuse & Neglect*, *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*, *Clinical Psychology Review*, *The Lancet*).
2. **Large nationally representative surveys** and prospective cohort studies (e.g., the Australian Child Maltreatment Study, the National Survey of Children's Exposure to Violence, the National Incidence Studies).
3. **Official statistical systems and government reports** (CDC, FBI, DOJ/BJS, NCMEC CyberTipline, WHO, UNICEF, national statistical offices).
4. **Peer-reviewed single studies**, including qualitative studies and dissertations, used for mechanism and description and labeled as such.
5. **Expert and practitioner commentary** (e.g., NCMEC behavioral-analysis monographs, Royal Commission reports), used for practice guidance and clearly distinguished from empirical estimates.
6. **Journalism, advocacy content, and social media**, used only as *objects of study* — to trace the origin of a claim — never as evidence for the claim itself.

Where a claim rests only on levels 4–6, it is labeled **Emerging** or **Unsupported**, not **Established**.

## 3. Search strategy

Searches combined terms for the outcome (child sexual abuse, child molestation, child sexual exploitation, grooming, online enticement, sextortion, child sexual abuse material) with terms for each domain (victim selection, risk factors, offender characteristics, cognitive distortion, pedophilia, prevention, disclosure, recidivism).

Sources were located through:

- PubMed/MEDLINE and PMC
- APA PsycINFO and PsycNET
- Google Scholar and publisher sites (Elsevier, Sage, Springer, Taylor & Francis, Wiley)
- Agency publications: CDC, NIJ, OJJDP, BJS, FBI, NCMEC, WHO, UNICEF, the Australian Institute of Criminology, the UK Centre of Expertise on Child Sexual Abuse, the Australian National Office for Child Safety
- Citation chasing from systematic reviews

This is a **structured narrative review**, not a PRISMA-registered systematic review. No meta-analysis was performed. Search results were screened by relevance and source quality; no formal dual-reviewer screening or risk-of-bias scoring was conducted. This is a material limitation and is stated plainly rather than disguised.

## 4. Evidence grading rubric

| Grade | Criteria | Example in this repository |
|---|---|---|
| **Established** | Replicated across independent samples and methods; supported by meta-analysis, systematic review, or consistent national surveillance; direction of effect stable across definitions | Most CSA is committed by known persons; grooming behaviors are reported by the large majority of survivors in specialized surveys |
| **Emerging** | One dataset, small samples, new instruments, qualitative designs, or a small number of studies with consistent direction; plausible mechanism but not replicated | Specific prevalence estimates for online grooming by adults; jurisdiction-specific trends in sextortion |
| **Disputed** | Studies conflict; measurement is contested; effects depend on definitions, sample type, or model specification | Whether cognitive distortions are causes, justifications, or post-hoc rationalizations; the strength of the link between childhood victimization and later offending |
| **Unsupported** | No credible empirical support, internally incoherent, or contradicted by better evidence | The "father under 200 pounds" selection rule; the idea that strangers commit most CSA; the idea that a child's silence proves nothing happened |

Grades describe the **strength of the evidence**, not the **importance of the claim**. A claim can be important and still be Emerging.

## 5. Handling the father-protection claim

Because this claim is the repository's explicit misinformation target, it was evaluated against four tests:

1. **Provenance:** Can the claim be traced to a named source, and does that source claim empirical support?
2. **Construct validity:** Is "father's body weight" measured, or is it a proxy for something else (presence, involvement, perceived threat, household income)?
3. **Empirical existence:** Is there any peer-reviewed study — case-control, cohort, or offender self-report — that records paternal weight and tests it against victim selection?
4. **Falsifiability and mechanism:** What mechanism would produce a 200-pound threshold, and would that mechanism predict a step function at that specific value?

A claim fails the evidentiary test if it cannot clear tests 1–3, regardless of how often it is repeated. Test 4 is used to distinguish a crude but possibly real effect (e.g., perceived guardianship) from a fabricated numerical rule.

## 6. Avoiding causal overreach

Three rules were applied throughout:

- **Correlation is labeled as correlation.** Family structure, disability, poverty, and prior victimization are *markers* of elevated statistical risk. They are not causes of an offender's behavior, and they do not excuse it.
- **Selection effects are made explicit.** Offender samples are drawn from people who were caught. Convicted samples overrepresent the poorest, least socially protected, and most visible offenders, and underrepresent offenders with institutional cover or private access.
- **Reverse causation is considered.** For example, a child who is isolated may be targeted *because* of that isolation; but abuse can also *produce* isolation. Cross-sectional data cannot distinguish these.

## 7. Preventing harm while describing harm

This repository describes grooming tactics at a functional level so that caregivers and professionals can recognize them. It deliberately omits:

- scripts, phrasing, or sequences that could be reused to groom a child
- instructions for avoiding detection, evidence handling, or manipulating disclosure
- technical guidance on producing, obtaining, or concealing abusive material
- operational security, encryption, or platform-evasion advice

Descriptions are aggregated across studies and paraphrased. They are not drawn from a single case in a way that would identify a victim.

## 8. Language and stigma standards

- "Person who sexually abused a child" / "offender" for behavior; "pedophilia" only for the clinical pattern of sexual interest.
- No group is described as inherently dangerous. Where prevalence differs by group, the text identifies structural and measurement explanations before any individual-level explanation.
- Victim behavior is never framed as provocation. Compliance, silence, delayed disclosure, and even affection toward an offender are described as survival and coercion dynamics, not consent.
- Statistical risk factors are paired with an explicit statement that they do not transfer responsibility to the child or family.

## 9. Limitations

Readers should weigh the following:

1. **Underreporting.** Official data capture a minority of abuse. CDC, BJS, and NCMEC all state that their counts underestimate prevalence.
2. **Definitional heterogeneity.** "Child sexual abuse" ranges from non-contact exposure to penetrative assault. Prevalence estimates differ by a factor of ten or more depending on definition and method (Stoltenborgh et al., 2011; Barth et al., 2013).
3. **Measurement effects.** Self-report surveys yield far higher prevalence than informant or official-record studies (Stoltenborgh et al., 2011: 127 vs. 4 per 1,000).
4. **Convicted-sample bias.** Most offender research uses incarcerated or forensic samples. Findings may not generalize to undetected offenders or to non-contact-only offending.
5. **Publication and funding bias.** Prevention and intervention research is disproportionately funded and published by program developers and advocacy organizations.
6. **Cultural and geographic skew.** Most high-quality studies come from the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, and Western Europe. Global estimates rely on heterogeneous national surveys.
7. **Temporal change.** Online offending patterns change faster than peer review. Figures for enticement and sextortion are trend indicators, not stable base rates.
8. **No full-text appraisal.** Citations were checked against publisher abstracts and, where accessible, full texts. This is not a substitute for independent risk-of-bias assessment.
9. **AI assistance.** Drafting and source organization were assisted by an AI system; claims were checked against the cited sources, but readers should verify any statistic that will be used in a decision with real consequences.

## 10. Corrections policy

Corrections are welcome and are treated as a feature, not an embarrassment. If a citation is wrong, a statistic is misstated, or a claim overreaches:

1. Open an issue with the claim, the source, and the proposed correction.
2. If the error is factual, it will be corrected and the commit will say so.
3. If the interpretation is contested, the repository will present the competing evidence rather than silently choosing a side.
4. Retracted or superseded sources will be removed or explicitly flagged.

See [`CONTRIBUTING.md`](CONTRIBUTING.md).

## 11. Ethical statement

Research on child sexual abuse carries a duty not to become a source of harm. This repository:

- does not identify victims or non-public offenders
- does not reproduce abusive content or link to it
- does not provide risk scores for named people
- directs readers to professional and crisis resources
- frames all findings toward protection, detection, and accountability

If you believe a child is in immediate danger, contact emergency services. In the United States, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline is **1-800-422-4453** and NCMEC is **1-800-THE-LOST**.
