# Annotated Bibliography

Every source cited in this repository appears below with a short annotation describing what it supports and how far it can be pushed. Links were checked at the time of compilation (November 2026). Some publisher pages require institutional access; where a free full text or abstract is available, that link is given.

**How to read the annotations:** "Use for" states the claim the source can support. "Caution" states the limitation that should travel with it.

---

## A. Prevalence, incidence, and epidemiology

**Barth, J., Bermetz, L., Heim, E., Trelle, S., & Tonia, T. (2013). The current prevalence of child sexual abuse worldwide: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *International Journal of Public Health, 58*, 469–483.**
Systematic review documenting enormous between-study variation (0–69% for girls, 0–47% for boys) and the dependence of estimates on definitions and methods.
Use for: the claim that prevalence estimates are definition-dependent and should be reported as ranges.
Caution: cited here via the *Lancet* review because the original is paywalled; the range is a summary of heterogeneity, not a pooled estimate.

**CDC (2024, updated 2026). About Child Sexual Abuse.**
Authoritative U.S. summary: at least 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys experience CSA; about 90% is perpetrated by someone known and trusted; lifetime economic burden at least $9.3 billion (2015).
Use for: headline U.S. prevalence, the known-offender finding, and cost.
Caution: the page notes that underreporting means these figures underestimate the problem; an earlier CDC report used 1 in 13 boys, reflecting different definitions and sources.

**CDC (2024). Risk and Protective Factors for Child Abuse and Neglect.**
Lists individual, family, and community risk factors, including non-biological caregivers in the home, parental substance use, social isolation, and economic stress.
Use for: the socio-ecological framing of risk.
Caution: this page concerns child abuse and neglect generally, not CSA specifically; do not present its items as CSA-specific odds ratios.

**CHILD USA (2024). Delayed Disclosure.**
Factsheet summarizing disclosure research: over 70% do not disclose within five years; about 1 in 5 never disclose; average delay around 20 years; DOJ estimate that 86% went unreported before adulthood.
Use for: the claim that delayed and non-disclosure are the norm.
Caution: a advocacy-organization synthesis of primary studies, not a new empirical study; verify individual statistics against the cited primary sources where precision matters.

**CSA Centre (2021). The scale and nature of child sexual abuse: Review of evidence.**
Comprehensive U.K. review, including ONS Table 12b on perpetrator relationship for contact CSA.
Use for: U.K. prevalence and the perpetrator-relationship distribution (father 5.4%, stepfather 6.3%, acquaintance 37.7%, stranger 21.0%).
Caution: respondents are adults recalling childhood; multiple perpetrators mean percentages exceed 100.

**CSA Centre (2026). The scale and nature of child sexual abuse: Review of evidence (2026 update).**
Updates official trends through 2024/25 and includes age-specific prevalence estimates from recent global analyses.
Use for: current U.K. trends and age-band prevalence.
Caution: official data reflect reporting and recording changes as well as incidence.

**Gewirtz-Meydan, A., & Finkelhor, D. (2019). Sexual abuse and assault in a large national sample of children and adolescents. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*.**
National Survey of Children's Exposure to Violence (n = 13,052): most offenses by other juveniles (76.7% for male victims, 70.1% for female victims); 66.3% of episodes not reported to an adult; police reports in 19.1%.
Use for: peer offending, disclosure rates, and the adolescent skew in community samples.
Caution: broad definition of "sexual abuse and assault," including peer and non-contact incidents; not directly comparable to clinical or legal definitions.

**Mathews, B., et al. (2024). Child sexual abuse by different classes and types of perpetrator: Prevalence and trends from an Australian national survey. *Child Abuse & Neglect*.**
Australian Child Maltreatment Study (n = 8,503): lifetime CSA prevalence 28.5%; prevalence by perpetrator class; declines in abuse by parents/caregivers and other known adults, increases in abuse by adolescent romantic partners.
Use for: national prevalence and perpetrator-class breakdown outside the U.S.
Caution: Australian sample; definitions and survey method affect comparability.

**Mathews, B., et al. (2025). Disclosure and non-disclosure of childhood sexual abuse in Australia: Results from a national survey. *Child Abuse & Neglect*.**
Lifetime disclosure 54.8%, non-disclosure 45.2%; disclosure higher among women and younger cohorts.
Use for: the claim that a large minority never disclose.
Caution: self-report; "disclosure" includes informal disclosure, not only official reporting.

**Pereda, N., Guilera, G., Forns, M., & Gómez-Benito, J. (2009). The prevalence of child sexual abuse in community and student samples: A meta-analysis. *Clinical Psychology Review, 29*, 328–338.**
Pooled prevalence of 19.7% for girls and 7.9% for boys across 65 studies in 22 countries.
Use for: global community-sample prevalence estimates.
Caution: community and student samples, not high-risk or clinical populations.

**Stoltenborgh, M., van IJzendoorn, M. H., Euser, E. M., & Bakermans-Kranenburg, M. J. (2011). A global perspective on child sexual abuse: Meta-analysis of prevalence around the world. *Child Maltreatment, 16*, 79–101.**
331 samples, 9.9 million participants: self-reported CSA 127 per 1,000 overall (180 girls, 76 boys); informant studies 4 per 1,000.
Use for: the best single demonstration that method drives prevalence estimates.
Caution: high heterogeneity; regional estimates vary widely.

**UNICEF (2026). Sexual violence data.**
Estimates that about 650 million girls and women alive today (1 in 5) experienced childhood sexual violence, and 410–530 million boys and men (around 1 in 7).
Use for: global scale.
Caution: modeled from heterogeneous national surveys; not a precise count.

**WHO (2026). Child maltreatment fact sheet.**
One in 5 women and 1 in 7 men report having been sexually abused as a child; child maltreatment is often hidden and only a fraction of victims receive services.
Use for: global prevalence and the hidden-victimization framing.
Caution: broad child maltreatment context; sexual abuse figures rely on national surveys.

***The Lancet* (2025). Prevalence of sexual violence against children and age at first exposure: a global analysis by location, age, and sex (1990–2023).**
Recent global analysis confirming substantial regional variation and earlier pooled estimates of roughly 18% for girls and 7.6% for boys.
Use for: current global comparative context.
Caution: modeling study; estimates depend on included surveys and definitions.

**RAINN. Sexual violence statistics.**
Cites U.S. child protective services data (about 56,575 children sexually abused per year) and FBI data (51% of reported rape victims are ages 0–19).
Use for: official-case counts and the age distribution of reported rape.
Caution: substantiated cases only; a detection count, not incidence.

## B. Victim selection, relationships, and settings

**AASAS (2014). Child Sexual Abuse: A Cursory Review of Risk and Protective Factors for Victimization and Perpetration.**
Reviews family-structure associations: living with one parent, stepfather presence (roughly doubled risk for girls), and not living with natural parents.
Use for: the claim that household composition is associated with elevated risk.
Caution: associations are confounded with income, stress, and access; the review does not establish causation.

**Assini-Meytin, L. C., McPhail, I., Sun, Y., Mathews, B., Kaufman, K. L., & Letourneau, E. J. (2025). Child sexual abuse and boundary-violating behaviors in youth-serving organizations. *Child Maltreatment*.**
National survey: 3.75% of adults experienced CSA in a youth-serving organization; younger cohorts reported a lower proportion in major national youth organizations (29.1% vs. 44.5%).
Use for: organizational prevalence and suggestive evidence of prevention effects.
Caution: retrospective self-report; cohort differences may reflect reporting as well as incidence.

**Australian Institute of Criminology (2015). Adult sex offenders in youth-oriented institutions: Evidence on sexual victimisation experiences of offenders and their offending patterns (T&I 497).**
Includes a small sample (n = 16) of offenders describing victim selection: all targeted children perceived to know about sex; 37.5% described the child as well supervised.
Use for: illustrating that supervision alone does not explain selection, and that offenders exploit perceived sexual precocity.
Caution: tiny, convicted, qualitative sample; not generalizable.

**Australian Institute of Criminology (2014). Profiling parental child sex abuse (T&I 465).**
Describes parental offenders: 55% non-biological fathers, 45% biological fathers; differences between the groups were negligible in the analyzed sample.
Use for: the claim that biological fathers are also perpetrators and that the biological/non-biological distinction is not a clean profile.
Caution: treatment-referral sample, not a population sample.

**CUNY (2025). Preventing Sexual Violence Where It Most Often Occurs: An Investigation of the Situational and Structural Components of Child Sexual Abuse in Residential Settings (dissertation).**
Reports that 54.1% of offenders were selected because the guardian trusted them; 70.9% of offenders said they did not know what made the victim appear vulnerable.
Use for: guardian trust as an access mechanism; the limits of offender "selection" narratives.
Caution: single dissertation; not peer-reviewed in the same sense as a journal article.

**Ebberline, J., & Holmberg, U. (2008). Child Molesters and Children as Witnesses (Lund University dissertation).**
Swedish modus-operandi research finding that non-familial child molesters tend to offend in their own home or close to it.
Use for: the residential-location finding.
Caution: small Swedish sample; spatial patterns may differ elsewhere.

**Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor (2019).** See §A. Also the best single U.S. source for the juvenile-offender majority and low reporting rates.

**Lanning, K. V. (2010). Child Molesters: A Behavioral Analysis (8th ed.). NCMEC/OJJDP.**
The standard practitioner monograph on offender behavior, typologies, access, and acquaintance molestation. Explicitly warns that offenders are not identifiable by physical traits and that incest offenders can be preferential offenders.
Use for: the acquaintance-molestation finding, access-based prevention, and the limits of profiling.
Caution: practitioner synthesis, not a prevalence study; typologies are descriptive.

**Mathews et al. (2024).** See §A. Perpetrator-class prevalence table.

**ONS (2020), Table 12b, in CSA Centre (2021).** See §A. Perpetrator-relationship distribution.

**Crime Science (2015). How victim age affects the context and timing of child sexual abuse: applying the routine activities approach to the first sexual abuse incident.**
Offender sample: mean age at first incident 10.45 years; 53% familial first victim; 66% knew the victim for more than a year; 6% stranger-onset.
Use for: age distribution and the routine-activities framing of access.
Caution: offender sample; age and relationship patterns may not generalize to undetected offenders.

**Hatay study (2016). General Characteristics of Child Sexual Offenders in Hatay, Turkey. *Journal of Forensic Sciences* (PMC4792499).**
Small clinical sample (n = 48): 91.7% knew the victim; 52.1% of victims lived with both parents.
Use for: countering the assumption that abuse is confined to broken families.
Caution: small, regional, clinical sample; not a prevalence estimate.

## C. Offender psychology, risk, and recidivism

**Blanchard, R., et al. (2001); Schaefer, G. A., et al. (2010).** Cited in Seto (2018) and in the neurobiology review below. Roughly half of men who sexually abuse children are not pedophilic.
Use for: the pedophilia–offending distinction.
Caution: secondary citation here; consult the originals for diagnostic methodology.

**Cortoni, F., & Hanson, R. K. (2005); Cortoni, F. (2010); McLeod, D. (2015); Sandler, J. C., & Freeman, N. J. (2007).** Sources reviewed in "Female perpetrators of child sexual abuse: A 20-year update" (2022).
Estimates that 5–20% of CSA is perpetrated by female offenders.
Use for: the prevalence range for female perpetration.
Caution: estimates vary with definitions and samples; female-perpetrated abuse is likely under-recognized.

**Finkelhor, D. (1984). *Child Sexual Abuse: New Theory and Research*. Free Press.**
The Four Preconditions Model: motivation, overcoming internal inhibitions, overcoming external inhibitions, and overcoming the child's resistance.
Use for: the theoretical basis of opportunity-focused prevention.
Caution: a framework, not a validated causal model.

**Gannon, T. A., & Polaschek, D. L. L. (2006). Cognitive distortions in child molesters: A re-examination of key theories and research. *Clinical Psychology Review, 26*(8), 1000–1019.**
Concludes that clinical practice has outpaced evidence on cognitive distortions and that most research cannot establish their causal role.
Use for: the "Disputed" grade on cognitive distortions.
Caution: a critical review, not a claim that cognitions are irrelevant.

**Hanson, R. K., & Bussière, M. T. (1998). Predicting relapse: A meta-analysis of sexual offender recidivism studies. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*.**
Meta-analysis: 13.4% sexual recidivism over 4–5 years; 12.7% for child molesters.
Use for: base rates.
Caution: official recidivism only; underestimates true reoffending.

**Hanson, R. K., & Morton-Bourgon, K. E. (2004). Predictors of sexual recidivism: An updated meta-analysis. Public Safety Canada.**
95 studies, 31,216 offenders: 13.7% sexual recidivism over 5–6 years; deviant sexual interest and antisocial orientation are the strongest predictors.
Use for: base rates and risk-factor structure.
Caution: 5–6 year average follow-up; longer follow-up yields higher cumulative rates.

**Hanson, R. K., Lee, S. C., & Thornton, D. (2024). Long-term recidivism rates among individuals at high risk to sexually reoffend. *Sexual Abuse*.**
Reviews 5-, 10-, and 20-year recidivism, noting recent routine samples of 2–8% and the importance of base rates.
Use for: the claim that risk is heterogeneous and time-dependent.
Caution: high-risk samples are not representative of all offenders.

**Langan, P. A., Schmitt, E. L., & Durose, M. R. (2003). Recidivism of sex offenders released from prison in 1994. U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics.**
5.1% of child molesters were rearrested for a new sex crime within three years; 39.4% for any crime.
Use for: U.S. recidivism base rates.
Caution: three-year follow-up; rearrest is a conservative measure.

**Lussier, P., McCuish, E., Chouinard Thivierge, S., & Frechette, J. (2023). A meta-analysis of trends in general, sexual, and violent recidivism among youth with histories of sex offending. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*.**
Sexual recidivism among youth has been declining; general recidivism remains higher.
Use for: the juvenile-offender section.
Caution: heterogeneity in samples and follow-up.

**Mann, R. E., Hanson, R. K., & Thornton, D. (2010). Assessing risk for sexual recidivism: Some proposals on the nature of psychologically meaningful risk factors. *Sexual Abuse*.**
Organizes dynamic risk factors: sexual preoccupation, deviant interest, intimacy deficits, antisociality, and poor self-regulation.
Use for: risk-factor structure in treatment and supervision.
Caution: dynamic factors are treatment targets, not a public profile.

**Seto, M. C. (2018). *Pedophilia and Sexual Offending Against Children: Theory, Assessment, and Intervention* (2nd ed.). American Psychological Association.**
The standard scholarly treatment of the distinction between pedophilia and sexual offending, prevalence, assessment, and intervention.
Use for: definitions and the ~50% overlap estimate.
Caution: a book-length synthesis; individual prevalence figures vary by sample.

**Soldino, V., & Seigfried-Spellar, K. C. (2024). Criminological differences between contact-driven and online-focused suspects in online child sexual grooming police reports. *Child Abuse & Neglect, 149*, 106696.**
Contact-driven suspects used more positive rapport, sent unprompted sexual images, and offered exchanges more often than online-focused suspects.
Use for: the heterogeneity of online offenders.
Caution: police-report sample; detection bias.

**Ward, T., & Beech, A. R. (2006). An integrated theory of sexual offending. *Aggression and Violent Behavior*.**
Integrates biological, ecological, and psychological factors.
Use for: the multifactorial framing.
Caution: a comprehensive theory, not a predictive model.

**Ward, T., & Keenan, T. (1999). Child molesters' implicit theories. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*.**
Describes implicit theories about children as sexual beings, dangerousness, and entitlement.
Use for: offense-supportive belief content.
Caution: clinical/qualitative origins; not a diagnostic taxonomy.

**Ward, T., & Siegert, R. J. (2002). Toward a comprehensive theory of child sexual abuse: A theory knitting perspective. *Psychology, Crime & Law*.**
Pathways model: intimacy deficit, deviant sexual scripts, emotional dysregulation, antisocial cognitions, and multiple mechanisms.
Use for: the claim that there is no single offender pathway.
Caution: pathways are not cleanly separable empirically.

***A meta-analysis of recidivism rates among individuals who commit child sexual exploitation material (CSEM) offending* (2026).**
30 samples, N = 25,978: 5.9% any sexual recidivism, 1.5% contact sexual recidivism after ~5 years; mixed offenders 8.8% contact vs. 0.6% CSEM-exclusive.
Use for: differentiated risk in online offending.
Caution: official records; CSEM-exclusive status is detection-dependent.

**Pedophilia prevalence sources: Dombert, B., et al. (2016); Joyal, C. C., et al. (2015); Santtila, P., et al. (2015); Winters, G. M., et al. (2023).** Reviewed in "Assessing Pedophilic Sexual Interest" (2025).
General-population pedophilic interest estimated at roughly 1–5%; among child-victim offenders, 27–70%.
Use for: the prevalence range.
Caution: measurement varies (self-report, viewing time, phallometry); ranges are wide for a reason.

## D. Grooming and online offending

**Berens, K. A., Bruer, K. C., Schick, K. D., Evans, A. D., & Price, H. L. (2023). A taxonomy of groomer profiles: Comparisons of in-person, online, and mixed groomers through the examination of Canadian judicial decisions. *Child Abuse & Neglect, 145*, 106407.**
180 judicial decisions: mixed groomers used more strategies and identified more vulnerable victims; in-person victims were younger and abused longer.
Use for: the in-person/online/mixed distinction.
Caution: judicial decisions capture prosecuted cases, not all grooming.

**Child Abuse & Neglect (2022). A scoping review of child grooming strategies: pre- and post-internet.**
93 papers: pre-internet strategies (enticements, coercion, isolation, substance abuse, gradual sexualization, secrecy) and post-internet additions (risk assessment, fantasy, media progression, meeting, deception, repetition).
Use for: the continuity and change in grooming tactics.
Caution: scoping review, not a prevalence study.

**Elliott, I. A. (2017). A self-regulation model of online sexual grooming.**
Two-phase model: potentiality (rapport-building, incentivization, disinhibition, security management) and disclosure, with strategies adjusting to victim feedback.
Use for: the dynamic, feedback-driven nature of online grooming.
Caution: theoretical model; empirical support is developing.

**Fry, D., Krzeczkowska, A., Ren, J., Lu, M., Fang, X., & Into the Light Index Study Group (2025). Prevalence estimates and nature of online child sexual exploitation and abuse: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, 9*(3), 184–193.**
86 records, 123 studies: past-year OCSEA 8.1%; online solicitation 12.5%; image-based 12.6%; exploitation 4.7%; extortion 3.5%.
Use for: global online prevalence.
Caution: high heterogeneity; definitions vary across studies.

**NCMEC (2024). 2024 CyberTipline Report.**
20.5 million reports (29.2 million incidents); 546,000 online enticement reports (+192%); 26,823 trafficking reports (+55%); nearly 100 financial sextortion reports per day; 36+ teenage boys known to have died by suicide after sextortion.
Use for: platform reporting volume and sextortion severity.
Caution: reporting volume is affected by law, platform behavior, and bundling; not a direct measure of incidence.

**NCMEC (2025). 2025 CyberTipline Report.**
21.3 million reports; 1,413,347 online enticement; 113,530 child sex trafficking; 53,000+ urgent escalations.
Use for: current enticement and trafficking reporting trends.
Caution: same reporting caveats; 2025 figures reflect the REPORT Act's expanded mandatory reporting.

**NCMEC (2026). New sextortion data: over 100 reports received daily in 2025.**
50,000+ financial sextortion reports in 2025 (137/day), up from 36,000+ in 2024; 94% of public reports from the child or parent when relationship known.
Use for: sextortion growth and reporting behavior.
Caution: reports, not unique victims; platform reporting dominates totals.

**UNH Crimes Against Children Research Center (2023). Online Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse: Findings from a National Survey.**
One in six respondents experienced online CSA before 18; about 30% of perpetrators were other juveniles; about a third of victims did not know the offender's identity.
Use for: the peer and acquaintance structure of online victimization.
Caution: self-report survey; definition includes non-contact experiences.

**Winters, G. M., Jeglic, E. L., & Kaylor, L. E. (2020). Validation of the sexual grooming model of child sexual abusers. *Journal of Child Sexual Abuse*.**
The five-stage Sexual Grooming Model and 77 behaviors.
Use for: the descriptive framework used in this repository.
Caution: a model, not a fixed sequence; offenders vary.

**Winters, G. M., Jeglic, E. L., Johnson, B. N., & Chou, C. (2024). The prevalence of sexual grooming behaviors among survivors of childhood sexual abuse. *Child Abuse & Neglect, 154*, 106842.**
n = 1,045 survivors: 99% endorsed at least one grooming behavior, mean 14.25; most common were charming presentation (70%), compliant/trusting child (68%), low self-esteem (61%), activities alone (57%), attention (56%), affection (54%), innocent touch (51%).
Use for: the phenomenology and frequency of grooming behaviors.
Caution: help-seeking survivor sample; retrospective; not a base rate.

**Wolak, J., Finkelhor, D., & Mitchell, K. (2008). Online "predators" and their victims: Myths, realities, and implications for prevention and treatment. *American Psychologist, 63*(2), 111–128.**
N-JOV findings: only 5% of offenders pretended to be teens; 73% of face-to-face victims had repeat encounters; most victims were adolescent girls; violence was rare.
Use for: correcting the online-predator stereotype.
Caution: arrested-offender sample; excludes undetected cases.

**Journal of Adolescent Health (2018). The prevalence of unwanted online sexual exposure and solicitation among youth: a meta-analysis.**
31 samples (37,649 participants) for exposure: mean 20.3%; 9 samples (18,272) for solicitation: 11.5%; rates have declined over time.
Use for: online exposure and solicitation prevalence.
Caution: heterogeneity and declining trend; definitions vary.

**NIJ (2022). Prevalence of online sexual offenses against children in the U.S.**
National survey (n = 2,639): lifetime online CSA 15.6%; image-based abuse 11.0%; self-produced images 7.2%; online grooming by adults 5.4%; sextortion 3.5%.
Use for: U.S. lifetime online estimates.
Caution: self-report; broad definitions.

**O'Connell, R. (2003). A typology of child cybersexploitation and online grooming practices.**
Six-stage online grooming typology from participant observation in chat rooms.
Use for: the historical online grooming model.
Caution: observational, dated; platforms have changed substantially.

## E. Prevention, safeguarding, and response

**CDC (2024). Prevention Resources for Action: Child Abuse and Neglect.**
Evidence-based prevention strategies at individual, family, community, and societal levels.
Use for: the public-health prevention frame.
Caution: broad maltreatment focus; CSA-specific effects vary.

**Gubbels, J., van der Put, C. E., Stams, G. J. J. M., et al. (2021). Effective components of school-based prevention programs for child abuse: A meta-analytic review. *Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review*.**
34 studies (158 effect sizes; N = 11,798) for knowledge and 22 studies (99 effect sizes; N = 7,804) for skills: d = 0.572 and d = 0.528; social-emotional skills, self-blame content, puppets, and games associated with larger effects.
Use for: school-program effectiveness on knowledge and skills.
Caution: knowledge and skills, not measured victimization.

**Kaufman, K. L., Erooga, M., Mathews, B., & McConnell, E. (2019). Recommendations for preventing child sexual abuse in youth-serving organizations. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*.**
Synthesizes Royal Commission findings and the literature into organizational prevention recommendations.
Use for: situational prevention in organizations.
Caution: recommendations are evidence-informed rather than evaluated as a package.

**Leclerc, B. (2026). A crime script analysis of child sexual abuse in youth-serving organisations. *Journal of Criminology*.**
Maps offender behavior across stages in youth-serving organizations and identifies intervention points.
Use for: crime-script-informed safeguarding.
Caution: incarcerated sample; script may not fit all settings.

**Little, T., et al. (2025). Interventions to prevent, reduce, and respond to violence against children and adolescents: a systematic review of systematic reviews to update the INSPIRE Framework.**
Rates school-based sexual abuse awareness programs and bystander interventions as not currently supported by sufficient evidence of violence reduction.
Use for: the honest caveat on school programs and bystanders.
Caution: a review of reviews; "not currently supported" means insufficient evidence, not evidence of harm.

**Lu, M., Barlow, J., Meinck, F., Walsh, K., & Wu, Y. (2023). School-based child sexual abuse interventions: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Research on Social Work Practice*.**
31 studies, 9,049 participants: knowledge g = 0.72 (questionnaire), g = 0.55 (vignette); more than three sessions and age 8+ associated with larger effects.
Use for: school-program knowledge effects and dosage.
Caution: knowledge outcomes; long-term behavioral effects unclear.

**NIJ (2024). Advancing situational and developmental approaches to prevent sexual violence.**
Describes situational prevention as "limited but promising," with structural and policy strategies showing potential in youth-serving organizations.
Use for: the evidence status of situational prevention.
Caution: a research agenda, not a program evaluation.

**National Office for Child Safety (Australia). Grooming; Who perpetrates child sexual abuse?; Child sexual abuse: Get the facts.**
Plain-language government guidance on grooming stages, signs, perpetrator heterogeneity, and prevention.
Use for: practice-oriented definitions and warning signs.
Caution: guidance documents, not primary research.

## F. The father-protection claim and its sources

**Guardian Training Solutions (2023). Lures of a Child Predator, Pt. 1.**
A trainer asserts that "every pedophile I've interrogated" would choose a different target if the child had an important father figure, and that an involved mother does not have the same effect.
Use for: documenting the anecdotal claim that circulates.
Caution: no data, no sample description, no weight threshold; the maternal claim is unsupported by the evidence reviewed here.

**KSNF/KODE (2023). Child predator's secrets are trending on social media.**
Reports the recirculation of a 2017 interview with convicted offender Jack Reynolds, including "If I thought the father was a threat, I would not approach the child."
Use for: tracing the viral claim.
Caution: journalism; one offender's self-report; "threat" is undefined and is not body weight.

**Podpage/Keep Kids Safe (2025). Involved Dads: A Predator's Worst Fear (podcast).**
ICAC investigator Kevin Cronister is described as having interviewed more than 100 pedophiles and as concluding that predators avoid children with present, emotionally available fathers.
Use for: documenting the practitioner claim.
Caution: unpublished, non-representative interviews; no coding or comparison group; no weight threshold.

**WRTV (2017). Convicted child molester says social media is giving more tools to predators.**
Original interview with Jack Reynolds, including his account of assessing family situations and avoiding fathers he perceived as a threat.
Use for: the primary journalistic source behind later viral claims.
Caution: one offender; self-report; no empirical test.

**All Pro Dad (2017); iMOM (2017). 6 Tactics of Child Predators.**
Popular articles describing offenders as targeting children in need, on the fringe, or craving attention.
Use for: illustrating how the "vulnerable child" narrative is popularized.
Caution: secondary, non-empirical; no weight claim.

**Police1 (2008). Child sexual offenders: How they select, manipulate and groom their victims.**
Practitioner article listing offender-reported victim characteristics: loneliness, quiet/passive temperament, attention-seeking, prior abuse, family dysfunction.
Use for: practitioner observation.
Caution: not a study; no sample or method; risk of victim-blaming framing.

**Guterman, N. B., & Lee, Y. (2005). The role of fathers in risk for physical child abuse and neglect. *Child Maltreatment, 10*(2), 136–149.**
Reviews pathways from father factors to physical abuse and neglect and notes how little is established about causal mechanisms.
Use for: cautioning against strong causal claims about father absence.
Caution: concerns physical abuse and neglect, not CSA specifically.

## G. Methods, definitions, and context

**American Psychological Association (2018). *Pedophilia and Sexual Offending Against Children* (2nd ed.) — introduction sample.**
Seto's explicit statement that some pedophilic individuals never offend and some offenders are not pedophilic.
Use for: the definitional distinction.
Caution: book introduction; cite the full book for detailed claims.

**Frontiers in Psychiatry (2025). Prevalence of pedophilia in a forensic psychiatric population in Switzerland.**
58.8% of 51 forensic examinees accused of sexual offenses against minors under 14 were diagnosed with pedophilia; contact offenses were more often intrafamilial.
Use for: illustrating the variability of pedophilia prevalence in forensic samples.
Caution: n = 51; forensic referral sample; not generalizable.

**Journal of Forensic Sciences / PMC (2016). General characteristics of child sexual offenders in Hatay, Turkey.**
See §B.

**SOTRAP (2025). Assessing pedophilic sexual interest: The utility of objective correlates in diagnostic decisions.**
Reviews prevalence estimates (1–5% general population; 27–70% among child-victim offenders) and assessment methods.
Use for: the prevalence range and measurement caveats.
Caution: review; estimates depend on assessment method.

**Childlight (2026). Into the Light Index: Data Update on Global Technology-Facilitated Child Sexual Exploitation and Abuse.**
Global survey estimates of past-year and lifetime online solicitation, CSAM exposure, exploitation, and extortion.
Use for: global online prevalence context.
Caution: survey-based; definitions and sampling vary by country.

**Thorn (2024). Trends in Financial Sextortion.**
