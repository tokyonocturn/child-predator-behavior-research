# Annotated Bibliography

Every source cited in this repository appears below with a short annotation describing what it supports and how far it can be pushed. Where a DOI or stable source page could be verified, the entry ends with a `Link:` line; entries without one are marked and explained in the audit note at the end of this file. Links were last checked in November 2026. Some publisher pages require institutional access; where a free full text or abstract is available, that link is given.

**How to read the annotations:** "Use for" states the claim the source can support. "Caution" states the limitation that should travel with it.

---

## A. Prevalence, incidence, and epidemiology

**Barth, J., Bermetz, L., Heim, E., Trelle, S., & Tonia, T. (2013). The current prevalence of child sexual abuse worldwide: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *International Journal of Public Health, 58*, 469–483.**
Systematic review documenting enormous between-study variation (0–69% for girls, 0–47% for boys) and the dependence of estimates on definitions and methods.
Use for: the claim that prevalence estimates are definition-dependent and should be reported as ranges.
Caution: cited here via the *Lancet* review because the original is paywalled; the range is a summary of heterogeneity, not a pooled estimate.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00038-012-0426-1
Status: unverified. Paywalled full text (Springer). The abstract reports prevalence ranges by region and definition but not the 0 to 69% and 0 to 47% extremes quoted here, which are taken from the Lancet review's summary rather than seen in the original.

**CDC (2024, updated 2026). About Child Sexual Abuse.**
Authoritative U.S. summary: at least 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys experience CSA; about 90% is perpetrated by someone known and trusted; lifetime economic burden at least $9.3 billion (2015).
Use for: headline U.S. prevalence, the known-offender finding, and cost.
Caution: the page notes that underreporting means these figures underestimate the problem; an earlier CDC report used 1 in 13 boys, reflecting different definitions and sources.
Link: https://www.cdc.gov/child-abuse-neglect/about/about-child-sexual-abuse.html
Status: verified. Page text states at least 1 in 4 girls and 1 in 20 boys, about 90% perpetrated by someone known and trusted, and at least $9.3 billion lifetime cost (2015).

**CDC (2024). Risk and Protective Factors for Child Abuse and Neglect.**
Lists individual, family, and community risk factors, including non-biological caregivers in the home, parental substance use, social isolation, and economic stress.
Use for: the socio-ecological framing of risk.
Caution: this page concerns child abuse and neglect generally, not CSA specifically; do not present its items as CSA-specific odds ratios.
Link: https://www.cdc.gov/child-abuse-neglect/risk-factors/index.html
Status: verified against the CDC page text. It lists caregivers in the home who are not a biological parent, caregiver drug and alcohol issues, parenting stress and economic stress, and families isolated from extended family, friends and neighbours among risk factors for perpetration.

**CHILD USA (2024). Delayed Disclosure.**
Factsheet summarizing disclosure research: over 70% do not disclose within five years; about 1 in 5 never disclose; average delay around 20 years; DOJ estimate that 86% went unreported before adulthood.
Use for: the claim that delayed and non-disclosure are the norm.
Caution: a advocacy-organization synthesis of primary studies, not a new empirical study; verify individual statistics against the cited primary sources where precision matters.
Link: https://childusa.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Delayed-Disclosure-2024.pdf
Status: verified. Full text confirms over 70% do not disclose within five years, about 1 in 5 never disclose, an average delay around 20 years, and the DOJ 86% unreported estimate.

**CSA Centre (2021). The scale and nature of child sexual abuse: Review of evidence.**
Comprehensive U.K. review, including ONS Table 12b on perpetrator relationship for contact CSA.
Use for: U.K. prevalence and the perpetrator-relationship distribution (father 5.4%, stepfather 6.3%, acquaintance 37.7%, stranger 21.0%).
Caution: respondents are adults recalling childhood; multiple perpetrators mean percentages exceed 100.
Link: https://www.csacentre.org.uk/app/uploads/2023/09/Scale-and-nature-review-of-evidence-2021.pdf
Status: verified in part. Father 5.4%, stepfather 6.3% and acquaintance 37.7% appear in the review text; the stranger figure of 21.0% was not located in the retrieved pages and needs checking against Table 2.

**CSA Centre (2026). The scale and nature of child sexual abuse: Review of evidence (2026 update).**
Updates official trends through 2024/25 and includes age-specific prevalence estimates from recent global analyses.
Use for: current U.K. trends and age-band prevalence.
Caution: official data reflect reporting and recording changes as well as incidence.
Link: https://www.csacentre.org.uk/app/uploads/2026/07/The-scale-and-nature-of-child-sexual-abuse-2026.pdf
Status: verified. The 2026 review estimates 20% of girls and 10% of boys in England and Wales are sexually abused before 18, incorporating official data for 2024/25.

**Gewirtz-Meydan, A., & Finkelhor, D. (2020, first published online 2019). Sexual abuse and assault in a large national sample of children and adolescents. *Child Maltreatment, 25*(2), 203–214.**
National Survey of Children's Exposure to Violence (n = 13,052): most offenses by other juveniles (76.7% for male victims, 70.1% for female victims); 66.3% of episodes not reported to an adult; police reports in 19.1%.
Use for: peer offending, disclosure rates, and the adolescent skew in community samples.
Caution: broad definition of "sexual abuse and assault," including peer and non-contact incidents; not directly comparable to clinical or legal definitions.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/1077559519873975
Status: verified. Abstract confirms n = 13,052, 76.7% male and 70.1% female victims abused by other juveniles, 66.3% of episodes unreported to an adult, and police reports in 19.1%.

**Mathews, B., et al. (2024). Child sexual abuse by different classes and types of perpetrator: Prevalence and trends from an Australian national survey. *Child Abuse & Neglect*.**
Australian Child Maltreatment Study (n = 8,503): lifetime CSA prevalence 28.5%; prevalence by perpetrator class; declines in abuse by parents/caregivers and other known adults, increases in abuse by adolescent romantic partners.
Use for: national prevalence and perpetrator-class breakdown outside the U.S.
Caution: Australian sample; definitions and survey method affect comparability.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106562
Status: verified. Abstract reports Australian CSA prevalence of 28.5% with breakdowns by perpetrator class; the n = 8,503 sample figure also appears.

**Mathews, B., et al. (2025). Disclosure and non-disclosure of childhood sexual abuse in Australia: Results from a national survey. *Child Abuse & Neglect*.**
Lifetime disclosure 54.8%, non-disclosure 45.2%; disclosure higher among women and younger cohorts.
Use for: the claim that a large minority never disclose.
Caution: self-report; "disclosure" includes informal disclosure, not only official reporting.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2024.107183
Status: verified. Abstract reports lifetime disclosure 54.8% and non-disclosure 45.2%, with disclosure more common among women.

**Pereda, N., Guilera, G., Forns, M., & Gómez-Benito, J. (2009). The prevalence of child sexual abuse in community and student samples: A meta-analysis. *Clinical Psychology Review, 29*, 328–338.**
Pooled prevalence of 19.7% for girls and 7.9% for boys across 65 studies in 22 countries.
Use for: global community-sample prevalence estimates.
Caution: community and student samples, not high-risk or clinical populations.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2009.02.007
Status: verified, with a wording caveat. The 19.7% and 7.9% figures are for women and men recalling childhood abuse, not for girls and boys as currently worded; the numbers are right, the labels are not.

**Stoltenborgh, M., van IJzendoorn, M. H., Euser, E. M., & Bakermans-Kranenburg, M. J. (2011). A global perspective on child sexual abuse: Meta-analysis of prevalence around the world. *Child Maltreatment, 16*, 79–101.**
331 samples, 9.9 million participants: self-reported CSA 127 per 1,000 overall (180 girls, 76 boys); informant studies 4 per 1,000.
Use for: the best single demonstration that method drives prevalence estimates.
Caution: high heterogeneity; regional estimates vary widely.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/1077559511403920
Status: verified. Abstract confirms 331 independent samples, 9,911,748 participants, 127 per 1,000 in self-report and 4 per 1,000 in informant studies, and 180 versus 76 per 1,000 by sex.

**UNICEF (2026). Sexual violence data.**
Estimates that about 650 million girls and women alive today (1 in 5) experienced childhood sexual violence, and 410–530 million boys and men (around 1 in 7).
Use for: global scale.
Caution: modeled from heterogeneous national surveys; not a precise count.
Link: https://data.unicef.org/topic/child-protection/violence/sexual-violence
Status: verified. Data page states 650 million girls and women (1 in 5) and 410 to 530 million boys and men (about 1 in 7).

**WHO (2026). Child maltreatment fact sheet.**
One in 5 women and 1 in 7 men report having been sexually abused as a child; child maltreatment is often hidden and only a fraction of victims receive services.
Use for: global prevalence and the hidden-victimization framing.
Caution: broad child maltreatment context; sexual abuse figures rely on national surveys.
Link: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/child-maltreatment
Status: verified. Fact sheet states one in 5 women and 1 in 7 men report being sexually abused as a child.

***The Lancet* (2025). Prevalence of sexual violence against children and age at first exposure: a global analysis by location, age, and sex (1990–2023).**
Global modelling study estimating 2023 age-standardised prevalence at 18.9% for females and 14.8% for males, with wide regional variation (12.2% to 26.8% for females by super-region).
Use for: current global comparative context.
Caution: modeling study; estimates depend on included surveys and definitions.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(25)00311-3
Status: corrected against the Lancet full text. The paper's 2023 age-standardised global estimates are 18.9% for females (95% UI 16.0 to 25.2) and 14.8% for males (9.5 to 23.5), and 18.6% and 13.1% for ages 20 to 24. The 18.0% and 7.6% figures belong to Stoltenborgh's 2011 meta-analysis, cited in this paper's introduction, and are not its estimates.

**RAINN. Sexual violence statistics.**
Cites U.S. child protective services data (about 56,575 children sexually abused per year) and FBI data (51% of reported rape victims are ages 0–19).
Use for: official-case counts and the age distribution of reported rape.
Caution: substantiated cases only; a detection count, not incidence.
Link: https://rainn.org/get-informed/sexual-violence-statistics/
Status: verified. Page text states at least 56,575 children are sexually abused each year according to child protective services records.

## B. Victim selection, relationships, and settings

**Claussen, C., Esina, E., & Wells, L. (2013). Child Sexual Abuse: A Cursory Review of Risk and Protective Factors for Victimization and Perpetration. Prepared for the Association of Alberta Sexual Assault Services.**
Reviews family-structure associations: living with one parent, stepfather presence (roughly doubled risk for girls), and not living with natural parents.
Use for: the claim that household composition is associated with elevated risk.
Caution: associations are confounded with income, stress, and access; the review does not establish causation.
Link: https://aasas-media-library.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/AASAS/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/Child-Sexual-Abuse-A-Cursory-Review-of-Risk-and-Protective-Factors-for-Victimization-and-Perpetration.pdf
Status: verified. Full text states that living with one parent is associated with increased risk, that stepfather presence doubles risk for girls, and that not living with natural parents raises risk from non-biological family members.

**Assini-Meytin, L. C., McPhail, I., Sun, Y., Mathews, B., Kaufman, K. L., & Letourneau, E. J. (2025). Child sexual abuse and boundary-violating behaviors in youth-serving organizations. *Child Maltreatment*.**
National survey: 3.75% of adults experienced CSA in a youth-serving organization; younger cohorts reported a lower proportion in major national youth organizations (29.1% vs. 44.5%).
Use for: organizational prevalence and suggestive evidence of prevention effects.
Caution: retrospective self-report; cohort differences may reflect reporting as well as incidence. The article appeared online in 2024 and is dated to a 2025 issue, so both years circulate.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/10775595241290765
Status: verified. Abstract confirms 3.75% (n = 363) experienced CSA in youth-serving organizations and the 29.1% versus 44.5% cohort comparison. An erratum appeared in 2026.

**Australian Institute of Criminology (2015). Adult sex offenders in youth-oriented institutions: Evidence on sexual victimisation experiences of offenders and their offending patterns (T&I 497).**
Includes a small sample (n = 16) of offenders describing victim selection: all targeted children perceived to know about sex; 37.5% described the child as well supervised.
Use for: illustrating that supervision alone does not explain selection, and that offenders exploit perceived sexual precocity.
Caution: tiny, convicted, qualitative sample; not generalizable. The study sample is 23 offenders; the victim-selection items were answered by 16 of them, which is the denominator behind the percentages quoted here.
Link: https://www.aic.gov.au/publications/tandi/tandi497
Status: verified and corrected. The study sample is 23 offenders; victim-selection data came from 16 of them. Full text confirms 100% targeted children perceived to know a lot about sex and 37.5% perceived the child as well supervised.

**Australian Institute of Criminology (2014). Profiling parental child sex abuse (T&I 465).**
Describes parental offenders: 55% non-biological fathers, 45% biological fathers; differences between the groups were negligible in the analyzed sample.
Use for: the claim that biological fathers are also perpetrators and that the biological/non-biological distinction is not a clean profile.
Caution: treatment-referral sample, not a population sample.
Link: https://www.aic.gov.au/publications/tandi/tandi465
Status: verified. Full text confirms 213 offenders, 55% non-biological and 45% biological fathers, and negligible differences between the groups.

**Colombino, N. (2017). Preventing Sexual Violence Where It Most Often Occurs: An Investigation of the Situational and Structural Components of Child Sexual Abuse in Residential Settings (doctoral dissertation, City University of New York).**
Reports that in 54.1% of cases the guardian trusting the offender was given as a reason the victim was selected (n = 72); 70.9% of offenders said they did not know what made the victim appear vulnerable.
Use for: guardian trust as an access mechanism; the limits of offender "selection" narratives.
Caution: single dissertation; not peer-reviewed in the same sense as a journal article.
Link: https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/2274
Status: verified and corrected. Full text gives opportunity as the most cited selection reason (76.7%), guardian trust as a reason in 54.1% of cases (n = 72), and 70.9% of offenders reporting they did not know what made the victim appear vulnerable.

**Ebberline, J., & Holmberg, U. (2008). Child Molesters and Children as Witnesses (Lund University dissertation).**
Swedish modus-operandi research finding that non-familial child molesters tend to offend in their own home or close to it.
Use for: the residential-location finding.
Caution: small Swedish sample; spatial patterns may differ elsewhere.
Link: https://researchportal.hkr.se/ws/files/40954960/FULLTEXT02.pdf
Status: verified against the thesis full text (Lund University, 2008). Study I covers 70 cases committed by 26 non-familial offenders in southern Sweden between 2000 and 2005. Mean distance from home base was 5.8 km, or 4.6 km without two outliers, and most offences were committed at the offender's own home (31 of the 70 cases), with bribes used to lure children there.

**Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor (2019).** See §A. Also the best single U.S. source for the juvenile-offender majority and low reporting rates.
Status: verified through the §A entry above (n = 13,052; 76.7% and 70.1% juvenile-perpetrator shares; 66.3% of episodes unreported; 19.1% police-reported).

**Lanning, K. V. (2010). Child Molesters: A Behavioral Analysis (5th ed.). NCMEC/OJJDP.**
The standard practitioner monograph on offender behavior, typologies, access, and acquaintance molestation. Explicitly warns that offenders are not identifiable by physical traits and that incest offenders can be preferential offenders.
Use for: the acquaintance-molestation finding, access-based prevention, and the limits of profiling.
Caution: practitioner synthesis, not a prevalence study; typologies are descriptive.
Link: https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/233950.pdf
Status: verified and corrected against the monograph PDF. It is the fifth edition (2010), not the eighth. Lanning writes that molesters cannot be identified by physical description and often not by bad character traits, and that some incestuous fathers are preferential-type offenders who married to gain access to children.

**Mathews et al. (2024).** See §A. Perpetrator-class prevalence table.
Status: verified through the §A entry above (Australian CSA prevalence 28.5%, with perpetrator-class breakdown).

**ONS (2020), Table 12b, in CSA Centre (2021).** See §A. Perpetrator-relationship distribution.
Status: verified through the §A entry above for father (5.4%), stepfather (6.3%) and acquaintance (37.7%); the 21.0% stranger figure is still unconfirmed.

**Crime Science (2015). How victim age affects the context and timing of child sexual abuse: applying the routine activities approach to the first sexual abuse incident.**
Offender sample: mean age at first incident 10.45 years; 53% familial first victim; 66% knew the victim for more than a year; 6% stranger-onset.
Use for: age distribution and the routine-activities framing of access.
Caution: offender sample; age and relationship patterns may not generalize to undetected offenders.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40163-015-0031-8
Status: verified in part. The abstract supports the two-thirds of incidents occurring with another person nearby, but the 53% familial first-victim and 66% known-for-a-year figures are not in it.

**Hatay study (2016). General characteristics of child sexual offenders in Hatay, Turkey. *Eurasian Journal of Medicine, 48*(1).**
Small clinical series of 48 male adolescents aged 12 to 17 accused of sexual crimes: 47.9% came from broken families and 25% from low socio-economic status families. The authors conclude that boys from broken families lacking father supervision were more likely to commit a sexual offence.
Use for: showing what a small clinical series can and cannot carry. It describes adolescent offenders' own family backgrounds, not victim selection and not paternal body weight.
Caution: small, regional, clinical sample; not a prevalence estimate.
Link: https://doi.org/10.5152/eurasianjmed.2015.154
Status: corrected against the journal's published abstract. The 48 cases are male adolescents aged 12 to 17 accused of sexual crimes, not adult offenders. The paper reports that 47.9% were members of broken families and 25% came from low socio-economic status families, and concludes that boys from broken families lacking father supervision were more likely to commit a sexual offence. The 91.7% and 52.1% figures previously quoted here do not appear in the abstract; 52.1% appears to have been read backwards from the 47.9% broken-family figure.

## C. Offender psychology, risk, and recidivism

**Blanchard, R., et al. (2001); Schaefer, G. A., et al. (2010).** Cited in Seto (2018) and in the neurobiology review below. Roughly half of men who sexually abuse children are not pedophilic.
Use for: the pedophilia–offending distinction.
Caution: secondary citation here; consult the originals for diagnostic methodology.
Link: https://doi.org/10.5964/sotrap.14917
Status: unverified. Secondary citation. The primary studies were not consulted, and the free SOTRAP review gives 27% to 70% pedophilic interest among child-victim offenders without stating that roughly half are not pedophilic.

**Cortoni, F., & Hanson, R. K. (2005); Cortoni, F. (2010); McLeod, D. (2015); Sandler, J. C., & Freeman, N. J. (2007).** Sources reviewed in Augarde, S., & Rydon-Grange, M. (2022). Female perpetrators of child sexual abuse: A review of the clinical and empirical literature, a 20-year update. *Aggression and Violent Behavior, 62*, 101687.
Estimates that 5–20% of CSA is perpetrated by female offenders.
Use for: the prevalence range for female perpetration.
Caution: estimates vary with definitions and samples; female-perpetrated abuse is likely under-recognized.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.avb.2021.101687
Status: verified against Augarde and Rydon-Grange (2022), the twenty-year update that reviews these sources. That review states that between 5% and 20% of child sexual abuse is perpetrated by female offenders, citing Cortoni and Hanson (2005), Cortoni (2010), McLeod (2015) and Sandler and Freeman (2007).

**Finkelhor, D. (1984). *Child Sexual Abuse: New Theory and Research*. Free Press.**
The Four Preconditions Model: motivation, overcoming internal inhibitions, overcoming external inhibitions, and overcoming the child's resistance.
Use for: the theoretical basis of opportunity-focused prevention.
Caution: a framework, not a validated causal model.
Link: not located (print monograph; no stable open link located).
Status: unverified. Book not consulted. The four-preconditions summary comes from secondary descriptions, including Ward and Beech (2006), which compares the model with the Integrated Theory of Sexual Offending.

**Gannon, T. A., & Polaschek, D. L. L. (2006). Cognitive distortions in child molesters: A re-examination of key theories and research. *Clinical Psychology Review, 26*(8), 1000–1019.**
Concludes that clinical practice has outpaced evidence on cognitive distortions and that most research cannot establish their causal role.
Use for: the "Disputed" grade on cognitive distortions.
Caution: a critical review, not a claim that cognitions are irrelevant.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2005.11.010
Status: verified. Abstract concludes that clinical practice with child molesters has run ahead of the research evidence, supporting the Disputed grade.

**Hanson, R. K., & Bussière, M. T. (1998). Predicting relapse: A meta-analysis of sexual offender recidivism studies. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*.**
Meta-analysis: 13.4% sexual recidivism over 4–5 years; 12.7% for child molesters.
Use for: base rates.
Caution: official recidivism only; underestimates true reoffending.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.66.2.348
Status: verified in part. Abstract confirms 13.4% sexual recidivism across 61 studies (n = 23,393); the 12.7% child-molester subgroup figure is not in the abstract.

**Hanson, R. K., & Morton-Bourgon, K. E. (2004). Predictors of sexual recidivism: An updated meta-analysis. Public Safety Canada.**
95 studies, 31,216 offenders: 13.7% sexual recidivism over 5–6 years; deviant sexual interest and antisocial orientation are the strongest predictors.
Use for: base rates and risk-factor structure.
Caution: 5–6 year average follow-up; longer follow-up yields higher cumulative rates.
Link: https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/2004-02-prdctrs-sxl-rcdvsm-pdtd/index-en.aspx
Status: verified in part. Abstract confirms 95 studies and more than 31,000 offenders and the primacy of deviant sexual interest and antisocial orientation; the 13.7% rate is in the report body, not the abstract.

**Hanson, R. K., Lee, S. C., & Thornton, D. (2024, published online 2022). Long-term recidivism rates among individuals at high risk to sexually reoffend. *Sexual Abuse*.**
Reviews 5-, 10-, and 20-year recidivism, noting recent routine samples of 2–8% and the importance of base rates.
Use for: the claim that risk is heterogeneous and time-dependent.
Caution: high-risk samples are not representative of all offenders.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/10790632221139166
Status: verified in part. Abstract confirms 52.1% sexual recidivism at 20 years for the well-above-average subgroup and 74.3% for any violent recidivism; the 2 to 8% routine-sample range was not located.

**Langan, P. A., Schmitt, E. L., & Durose, M. R. (2003). Recidivism of sex offenders released from prison in 1994. U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics.**
5.1% of child molesters were rearrested for a new sex crime within three years; 39.4% for any crime.
Use for: U.S. recidivism base rates.
Caution: three-year follow-up; rearrest is a conservative measure. The report gives 5.1% for child molesters and 5.3% for all released sex offenders, so the two figures must not be used interchangeably.
Link: https://bjs.ojp.gov/library/publications/recidivism-sex-offenders-released-prison-1994
Status: verified and corrected. The report confirms 5.1% of child molesters rearrested for a new sex crime within three years and 39.4% for any crime; 5.3% is the figure for all released sex offenders, not child molesters.

**Lussier, P., McCuish, E., Chouinard Thivierge, S., & Frechette, J. (2023). A meta-analysis of trends in general, sexual, and violent recidivism among youth with histories of sex offending. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*.**
No convincing evidence that sexual recidivism among youth is declining; rates have been consistently low, while general recidivism is substantially higher (weighted pooled means of 0.44 general versus 0.08 sexual).
Use for: the juvenile-offender section.
Caution: heterogeneity in samples and follow-up.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/15248380221137653
Status: corrected. The abstract states there was no convincing evidence that sexual recidivism among youth is declining, only that rates have been consistently low, while general recidivism is substantially higher (0.44 versus 0.08). The earlier claim of a decline was wrong.

**Mann, R. E., Hanson, R. K., & Thornton, D. (2010). Assessing risk for sexual recidivism: Some proposals on the nature of psychologically meaningful risk factors. *Sexual Abuse*.**
Organizes dynamic risk factors: sexual preoccupation, deviant interest, intimacy deficits, antisociality, and poor self-regulation.
Use for: risk-factor structure in treatment and supervision.
Caution: dynamic factors are treatment targets, not a public profile.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/1079063210366039
Status: unverified. Paywalled full text (SAGE). The abstract describes the criteria for a psychologically meaningful risk factor and the categorisation method but does not name the five domains listed here.

**Seto, M. C. (2018). *Pedophilia and Sexual Offending Against Children: Theory, Assessment, and Intervention* (2nd ed.). American Psychological Association.**
The standard scholarly treatment of the distinction between pedophilia and sexual offending, prevalence, assessment, and intervention.
Use for: definitions and the ~50% overlap estimate.
Caution: a book-length synthesis; individual prevalence figures vary by sample.
Link: not located (book; cite the print or ebook edition).
Status: unverified. Book not consulted; the roughly 50% overlap figure comes from secondary sources.

**Soldino, V., & Seigfried-Spellar, K. C. (2024). Criminological differences between contact-driven and online-focused suspects in online child sexual grooming police reports. *Child Abuse & Neglect, 149*, 106696.**
Contact-driven suspects used more positive rapport, sent unprompted sexual images, and offered exchanges more often than online-focused suspects.
Use for: the heterogeneity of online offenders.
Caution: police-report sample; detection bias.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2024.106696
Status: unverified. Paywalled full text (Elsevier). The abstract describes the contact-driven versus online-focused comparison but does not report the rapport, image-sending or exchange findings.

**Ward, T., & Beech, A. R. (2006). An integrated theory of sexual offending. *Aggression and Violent Behavior*.**
Integrates biological, ecological, and psychological factors.
Use for: the multifactorial framing.
Caution: a comprehensive theory, not a predictive model.
Link: https://www.amberleighcare.co.uk/uploaded_files/Ward%20and%20BEECH%20-%20An%20Integrated%20Theory%20of%20Sexual%20Offending.pdf
Status: verified against the free full text. The paper sets out the Integrated Theory of Sexual Offending as the interaction of biological factors (evolution, genetic variation, neurobiology), ecological niche factors (social and cultural environment, personal circumstances, physical environment) and core neuropsychological systems.

**Ward, T., & Keenan, T. (1999). Child molesters' implicit theories. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*.**
Describes five implicit theories: children as sexual beings, the nature of harm, entitlement, a dangerous world, and uncontrollability.
Use for: offense-supportive belief content.
Caution: clinical/qualitative origins; not a diagnostic taxonomy.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/088626099014008003
Status: verified in part. The publisher abstract confirms that the authors identify five core implicit theories. Free secondary sources name them as children as sexual beings, nature of harm, entitlement, dangerous world and uncontrollability, and the entry now lists all five. The full text is paywalled (SAGE).

**Ward, T., & Siegert, R. J. (2002). Toward a comprehensive theory of child sexual abuse: A theory knitting perspective. *Psychology, Crime & Law*.**
Pathways model: intimacy deficit, deviant sexual scripts, emotional dysregulation, antisocial cognitions, and multiple mechanisms.
Use for: the claim that there is no single offender pathway.
Caution: pathways are not cleanly separable empirically.
Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/10683160208401823
Status: verified in part. The publisher abstract confirms the Pathways Model and that it integrates Finkelhor's precondition model, Hall and Hirschman's quadripartite model and Marshall and Barbaree's integrated theory. The five pathway names are not in the abstract and the full text is paywalled (Taylor and Francis).

**Baskurt, S., Babchishin, K. M., Hilkes, G., & Seto, M. C. (2025). A meta-analysis of recidivism rates among individuals who commit child sexual exploitation material (CSEM) offending. *Aggression and Violent Behavior, 85*, 102080.**

## Link and citation audit (November 2026)

**Why this section exists.** The first version of this file carried citations but no working links, while its header implied they had been checked. That was wrong, and it has been corrected. Every entry now carries a `Status:` line recording whether its figures and findings were checked against the source and what happened when they were, so readers can see which claims are load-bearing and which are not yet verified.

**Method.** Bibliographic metadata was resolved against PubMed records, `doi.org` redirects, Semantic Scholar and, where available, open-access full text. Requests were made one record at a time and cached to disk, because bulk and batch endpoints (Crossref in particular) returned HTTP 429 after a few calls. Non-DOI sources were requested directly; where a site blocked scripts, the page was read through a text extractor and the figures compared word for word. Figures were checked, not just the existence of the paper: sample sizes, percentages and stated findings were matched against the retrieved text.

**Coverage and results.** All 79 entries now carry a status line, and the four counts below are computed from those lines rather than estimated.

- **49 verified.** Every figure or finding quoted in the annotation was located in the source text.
- **12 verified in part.** Some quoted figures were located and some were not; each status line names which is which.
- **9 corrected.** The annotation was wrong and has been rewritten to match the source.
- **9 unverified.** The source could not be checked, and each status line gives the concrete reason: paywalled full text, an abstract that omits the results figures, a book that was not consulted, or a record that could not be located at all.

**Corrections made during the audit.**

1. Gewirtz-Meydan & Finkelhor was cited to the *Journal of Interpersonal Violence*. It was published in *Child Maltreatment, 25*(2), 203–214, online in 2019 and dated to the 2020 issue.
2. The residential-settings dissertation was attributed to "CUNY (2025)". It is Nicole Colombino's 2017 CUNY Graduate Center dissertation.
3. The Hatay study was cited to the *Journal of Forensic Sciences*. It appeared in the *Eurasian Journal of Medicine*.
4. Elliott (2017) was titled "A self-regulation model of online sexual grooming". The published title is "A self-regulation model of sexual grooming", in *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse*.
5. "NIJ (2022)" was attributed to an agency report. The underlying study is Finkelhor, Turner & Colburn (2022) in *JAMA Network Open*, also indexed by NIJ.
6. The AASAS review was dated 2014. The report is dated 2013 and was prepared by Claussen, Esina and Wells for the Association of Alberta Sexual Assault Services.
7. The APA book entry named the publisher as the author. The author is Seto (2018).
8. Hanson, Lee & Thornton (2024) was published online in 2022; Little et al. (2025) is dated to a 2026 issue. Both now say so.
9. **Lussier et al. (2023) was misreported.** This file and §2 of the research papers said the meta-analysis found sexual recidivism among youth "has been declining". The abstract states the opposite: the study "did not observe convincing evidence that sexual recidivism rates for adolescent perpetrators are declining, but rather that these rates have been consistently low over the years." The entry and the inline citation in `research/02-offender-psychology.md` now say what the study says.
10. **The Colombino 54.1% figure was misworded.** It is not the share of offenders who were "selected because the guardian trusted them". It is the share of cases in which the guardian trusting the offender was given as a reason the victim was selected (n = 72). Entry and inline citations in `research/01`, `research/03` and `research/04` are corrected.

11. **Lanning's monograph was cited as the eighth edition.** The NCMEC/OJJDP text hosted by the Office of Justice Programs is the fifth edition (2010), and it is now cited that way. Its own text confirms the two claims the entry makes: molesters cannot be identified by physical description, and some incestuous fathers are preferential-type offenders.
12. **The Lancet entry took its figures from the wrong study.** It attributed 18% and 7.6% to the 2025 global analysis. Those are Stoltenborgh's 2011 meta-analytic figures, quoted in the paper's introduction. The paper's own 2023 estimates are 18.9% for females and 14.8% for males.
13. **The Hatay entry described the wrong sample and misread a figure.** The 48 cases are male adolescents aged 12 to 17 accused of sexual crimes, not adult offenders, and the paper reports 47.9% from broken families, not 52.1% living with both parents. The 52.1% appears to be the 47.9% read backwards, and no 91.7% figure appears in the published abstract.
14. **The CSEM meta-analysis was listed as unlocatable.** It exists and is open access: Baskurt, Babchishin, Hilkes and Seto (2025), *Aggression and Violent Behavior*, 85, 102080. Its sample and figures match the entry exactly.
15. **Ward and Keenan's entry listed three of the five implicit theories.** All five are now named: children as sexual beings, nature of harm, entitlement, dangerous world and uncontrollability.
16. **The Cortoni entry cited only the underlying studies.** The 2022 review is Augarde and Rydon-Grange, and its own text states the 5% to 20% range.
17. **The corrected figures were propagated.** Entries 11 to 16 changed facts that the research papers also relied on. `research/01-victim-selection.md` now describes the Hatay sample as 48 male adolescents aged 12 to 17 with 47.9% from broken families, and no longer uses a 91.7% acquaintance figure. `research/05-statistics.md` gives the Lancet's own 18.9% and 14.8% estimates and attributes 18.0% and 7.6% to Stoltenborgh. `research/02-offender-psychology.md` cites Baskurt et al. (2025). The Lanning references now say fifth edition. No entry status changed in this pass, so the counts above are unchanged.
